Chapter One

The Package

The box came on a Tuesday, in rain that couldn't decide whether to be rain or fog. It sat on the porch for an hour before David Kerrigan noticed it, because David was inside breaking a game about fishing.

The fishing game was called Reel Life Pro, and over the course of eleven days David had found four hundred and nine ways to break it. He had made a largemouth bass swim through the hull of a boat. He had made the in-game sun set twice in one evening. He had walked his angler off the edge of the lake and into a gray nowhere beneath the world, where the water was a flat plane seen from underneath and the trees hung upside down like roots. He wrote each of these up in the same flat, careful language: Steps to reproduce. Expected result. Actual result. Severity. He was good at it. He had been good at it for fifteen years, through three studios, a layoff, and a long, quiet slide into freelancing from the converted dining room of a narrow house on Bell Street in Vessey, Ohio.

Freelancing was the word he used. Hiding was the word his sister Claire used, on the phone, on Sundays.

At four-fifteen the doorbell camera chimed, which it did whenever a leaf crossed its field of view, and David glanced at the second monitor and saw the box. It was wrapped in brown paper and string. Not tape. String, knotted at the top in a neat bow, like a package in a picture book. He saved his report and went to the door.

The rain had soaked the paper dark at one corner. There was no shipping label, no barcode, only his name in black marker in an old-fashioned looping hand: Mr. D. Kerrigan, Quality Assurance. And beneath it, smaller: With compliments, Mott Interactive.

He carried it in and set it on the kitchen table. Logan's backpack was on the chair beside it, unzipped, a library book about rainforests slumping halfway out. Logan himself was upstairs; David could hear the faint, rhythmic thump of feet on floorboards, the sound of a boy wearing a headset and turning in circles in a world that wasn't there.

David cut the string with kitchen scissors.

Inside, nested in shredded green paper that looked like jungle grass, was a virtual-reality headset he didn't recognize. It was matte black, heavier than the consumer models, with a strap of soft gray fabric and a faceplate that had no logo at all. Beside it lay a pair of hand controllers, a charging cable, and an envelope of thick cream stock.

The letter inside was typed, though not on a printer. On a typewriter. The letters bit faintly into the paper.

Dear Mr. Kerrigan,

Your name was given to me by a colleague who described you as "the most patient man in testing." I am in need of patience. I have spent a long time building a small thing, and I would like it examined by someone careful before I let anyone else inside.

The enclosed device contains a pre-release build of BRAMBLE KINGDOM, a historical recreation of a regional amusement park that operated outside your own town between 1974 and 1989. I am told you live in Vessey. Perhaps you remember it.

I would like you to walk the park and report anything that seems wrong. I will pay your standard day rate for twenty days, and half again on completion. The first payment has already been sent.

Please test it alone. It is not ready for children.

Sincerely, W. Mott

David read the letter twice. Then he picked up his phone and checked his banking app, because in fifteen years in this business he had learned that the gap between already sent and sent could be wide enough to fall into.

The payment was there. Ten days of his rate, deposited that morning. The memo line said only BK.

"Huh," David said to the kitchen.

He remembered Bramble Kingdom. Everyone in Vessey over the age of forty remembered Bramble Kingdom, the way you remember a dream you had more than once. He'd gone when he was six, maybe seven. There had been a log flume that soaked you to the skin and a carousel of jungle animals instead of horses: zebras, tigers, a rhinoceros with a brass pole through its back. There had been a gorilla house. He remembered that clearly, the glass and the green dim inside, and his father lifting him up to see. He did not remember the gorillas themselves, only the feeling of being looked at by something enormous and patient through glass.

And the park had burned. He remembered that too, the way kids remember news: in fragments. Smoke on the horizon one autumn. His mother turning off the radio when he walked into the room. The ruins were still out there on Route 9, eight miles past the reservoir, fenced and posted and slowly being eaten by sumac and wild grape. Teenagers went out there to drink and scare each other. Nobody built on it. Nobody ever had.

He looked at the headset in its nest of green paper. It looked back at him with its blank black face.

Logan came down at six for dinner because David texted him, which was how they did things now. Food, the text said. Logan arrived three minutes later with his hair flattened in a ring where the headset strap had been, his eyes a little unfocused the way they always were for the first few minutes, as if the kitchen were the less convincing of two worlds.

He was twelve, tall for it, all elbows and wrists. He had his mother's eyes, a gray that went green in certain light, and her habit of tilting his head when he listened. David had learned to not look directly at him for too long. It was easier on both of them.

"Spaghetti," David said. "Again. I know."

"It's fine." Logan sat. He saw the box. "What's that?"

"Work."

"It's a headset." Logan was already half out of his chair. "That's not a Quest. That's not anything. Where's the logo?"

"There isn't one. Sit down. It's a contract."

"For what?"

David told him, briefly, because it was easier than not telling him. A historical park sim. A developer with a typewriter. Logan's eyes went wide and then narrow, the expression he'd had since he was very small when something was interesting enough to be suspicious.

"Bramble Kingdom," he said. "That's the burned-down place. Mom said they had gorillas there."

David's fork paused.

It happened like that, still, two years on. Nora would come into a sentence sideways, without warning, and the room would tilt a few degrees. She had worked at Halloway Primate Sanctuary for eleven years. She had known everything about gorillas, and she had told Logan all of it, at bedtime, in the car, over cereal. How they built nests every night. How a silverback could weigh four hundred pounds and still be gentle enough to groom a baby's eyebrows. How you should never, ever stare one in the eyes.

"She did," David said. "They had a gorilla house. I went there once when I was your age. Younger."

"Was it cool?"

"I don't really remember."

That was a lie, a small one. He remembered the glass and the green and the feeling of being looked at.

"Can I try it?"

"No."

"Dad."

"It says not for children. Right there in the letter. It's a pre-release build, it could have... it could do anything. Flicker, make you sick. Some of these early builds don't have comfort settings. You'll throw up."

"I never throw up."

"Logan."

Logan looked at his plate. He wound spaghetti around his fork with great care, the way Nora used to, and didn't eat it. The rain had become rain at last and ran down the kitchen window in long crooked lines.

"Fine," Logan said. Which in the language of twelve-year-olds meant nothing at all.

After Logan went back upstairs, after the dishes, after David had sat for a while in the living room with the television off and his phone in his hand not looking at anything, he went back to the kitchen and picked up the headset.

It was cold. That was the first thing. Not cool, the way plastic is cool, but cold, as if it had been sitting in a refrigerator rather than in a box on a porch. He turned it over. There was a single button on the left side. He pressed it and held it, and a small amber light woke beneath the faceplate, and somewhere deep inside the device a fan began to turn.

He carried it into the dining room, his office, and cleared a space on the floor by pushing back his chair. Testers learned to respect the physical world as much as the virtual one; he had once broken a toe on a filing cabinet during a sword-fighting game, and once walked straight into a door frame while being chased by a dinosaur. He put the controllers in his hands. He slid the headset down over his eyes.

Darkness first. Total, velvet darkness, the kind you never really get in the real world, where there's always light leaking from somewhere. Then a sound: crickets. Not a loop of crickets, the obvious four-second recording most developers used, but a real-sounding field of them, overlapping, irregular, near and far.

Then light. It came up slowly, as if a sun were rising inside his skull, and he was standing on a gravel path at the edge of a parking lot full of cars from the 1980s. Station wagons with wood paneling. A boxy brown sedan. A yellow pickup with a camper shell. The light was golden and long, late afternoon, and in front of him rose a gate.

It was enormous. Two wooden towers wrapped in fake vines, joined at the top by an arch of painted letters: BRAMBLE KINGDOM. Between the letters, carved and painted, monkeys swung from loop to loop. A toucan perched on the B. And at the very center of the arch, above the gap where the gates stood open, there was a face.

A gorilla's face, carved in wood, painted black and silver. Its eyes were closed.

David stood looking at it for a long moment. The detail was extraordinary. He could see the grain of the wood, the brush marks in the paint, a long crack that ran down through the gorilla's brow and had been filled with putty and painted over. He moved his head and the light shifted across the carving exactly as it should. He looked down at the gravel and saw individual stones, each with its own small shadow.

"Okay," he said aloud, softly. "Okay, W. Mott. That's very good."

He took a step forward. The gravel crunched in his headphones, a precise, satisfying crunch, and through the open gate he could see a wide plaza with a fountain and a carousel and banners strung between light poles. Music drifted out, faint and thin, a calliope playing something cheerful that he didn't recognize.

There were no people. That made sense; it was a test build. Still, the parking lot full of empty cars and the open gate and the music with nobody to hear it made the back of his neck prickle.

He walked through the gate. Nothing happened. He walked to the fountain, which was shaped like three stone elephants spraying water from their trunks, and he leaned over and saw his own reflection in the water. Not a reflection of himself: the game had given him a default avatar, a vague gray figure in a gray hooded jacket. But it was a reflection, and it moved when he moved, rippling. Reflections were expensive. Reflections were the sort of thing small studios didn't bother with.

He was beginning, despite himself, to be impressed.

He took off one controller strap and reached up to press the system button, intending only to check the build version and find the menu where he'd log his first notes. A plain panel slid into view in the air before him: BRAMBLE KINGDOM. Build 0.9.31. Session 1. Tester: KERRIGAN, D. Beneath that, a single option: CONTINUE.

No settings. No comfort options. No quit button.

He frowned and was reaching for the headset itself, to lift it off, when something moved at the far end of the plaza.

It was very small with distance, at the place where the main midway curved away out of sight behind a building painted to look like a jungle temple. A figure. A person, the first he'd seen, standing in the long golden light with one arm raised.

Waving.

David stood absolutely still. The figure was too far away to make out properly, but he could see enough. The outline of a ruffled collar, wide as a dinner plate. A round shape on the head that might have been a hat, or hair. A suit in two colors, divided down the middle. White gloves, startlingly white, the only crisp thing in that whole soft golden distance, moving slowly back and forth. Back and forth. Back and forth.

It was a clown.

The calliope music had stopped. He didn't know when. The crickets had stopped too. The plaza was silent except for the water in the elephant fountain, and the clown at the end of the midway kept waving, patient and slow, as if it had been waving for a very long time and could wait a great deal longer.

David found he had taken a step back.

He reminded himself that this was his job. That non-player characters were placed in scenes to draw players forward. That a clown at an amusement park was the least surprising thing in the world. He opened his mouth to say something to the empty room, a joke, the kind of joke he would have made to Nora, and found he had nothing to say.

He pulled the headset off.

His dining room. His chair. His two monitors, still showing the bug database for Reel Life Pro. The rain on the window. The house ticking quietly around him, as houses do.

He sat for a while with the headset in his lap. His heart was beating fast, which was ridiculous. He had been chased by dinosaurs. He had been eaten, in a variety of high-resolution ways, by zombies, sharks, and something called a Gloamwing. A clown waving from across a plaza was nothing.

Then he opened the cream envelope again and took out the second sheet he'd noticed earlier and not bothered to read: the build notes. A single typed page listing the attractions included in version 0.9.31. The gate. The plaza. The elephant fountain. The carousel. The log flume, marked (water shader incomplete). The midway games. The gorilla house, marked (closed in this build: do not attempt entry).

At the bottom of the page, under a heading that said CHARACTERS, there was a single line.

None in this build.

David read it three times. Then he folded the page very neatly, put it back in the envelope, and set the headset on the highest shelf in the dining room, between a box of old cables and a framed photograph of a woman laughing with a baby gorilla's hand wrapped around her finger.

Upstairs, the thumping of feet on the floorboards had stopped. The house was very quiet.

He went up to check on Logan and found him asleep on top of his covers, his own headset still on his face, its little light pulsing slow and blue, like something breathing.

Chapter Two

Admission

By morning the clown had become a bug.

That was how David's mind worked, how it had been trained to work. In the gray light of Wednesday, with coffee and the radio murmuring about a school-levy vote, the figure at the end of the midway was no longer a thing that had frightened him. It was a discrepancy between documentation and behavior. Expected result: no characters present. Actual result: one character present, distant, animated (waving loop). Severity: low. Note: build notes may be out of date.

He typed it into a fresh text file, because Mott hadn't given him a bug database, and he felt better with every word. Words did that. They boxed things in.

He got Logan off to school at seven-forty: cereal, the hunt for one missing shoe, the ritual exchange at the door.

"Got your lunch?"

"Yeah."

"Got your phone?"

"Yeah."

"Got your..."

"Dad. I'm twelve."

The door closed. Through the window David watched him walk down Bell Street toward the corner where the bus stopped, shoulders hunched, hood up against the drizzle, headphones on. He didn't look back. He never looked back anymore. When he was small, five or six, he used to turn at the corner and wave both arms over his head like a man flagging down a plane, and Nora would wave back from the porch with both of hers, and David, usually, would be inside on a call.

He turned away from the window.

✦

The second session began at nine. This time he did it properly. He moved the dining table against the wall and marked out a play space on the rug with blue painter's tape. He set a timer on his phone for ninety minutes, standard practice, because time in a headset went strange and you could lose an afternoon before you knew it. He opened his notes file on the laptop and propped it where he could reach it. Then he took the headset down from the shelf.

It was cold again. Colder, if anything. He held it against his cheek for a moment, frowning, then shrugged and put it on.

The parking lot. The cars. The golden light, as if no time had passed at all; as if the park had been waiting for him exactly where he'd left it, holding its breath. The crickets were back, and so was the calliope music drifting from inside. He looked up at the arch. The carved gorilla's eyes were still closed.

He walked through the gate.

This time there were people.

Not many, and not close. Shapes at the edges, ghost-pale and softly blurred, the way crowds appear in old photographs when the exposure was too long and anyone moving became a smear. A family by the carousel, two adults and two children, translucent as breath on a window. A teenage couple on a bench sharing something from a paper cone. A man in a striped uniform pushing a broom. They moved in short loops, a few seconds each, and when David walked toward them they faded before he could reach them, the way a mirage recedes on a hot road.

Background population. Low-detail ambient characters, intended to make a space feel lived-in. He'd seen it done a hundred ways. He'd never seen it done quite like this, so that the people seemed less like figures than like memories of figures, and he wondered how Mott had done it. From old photographs, maybe. Scanned and animated. It was the kind of thing that would win a small award at a small festival.

Note, he said to himself. Ambient crowd present, contrary to build notes. Very effective. Slightly unsettling. He'd write slightly. He wouldn't write the other thing, which was that each time one of the ghost-people faded, he had the distinct impression it had turned its head to look at him first.

He walked the plaza. He checked the fountain, the reflections, the shadows. He walked to the carousel and found he could climb onto it, onto a painted zebra with a gilded mane, and that when he did, the carousel began to turn and the calliope music swelled around him and the park revolved past: the jungle temple, the midway, the flume's wooden chute, a distant dome of green glass on a little hill.

The gorilla house. He knew it at once. The green glass, the shape of it. His father's hands under his arms.

He got off the carousel and walked toward the midway, testing as he went: brushing the controllers against walls to see if his hands would pass through, crouching to look under benches, jumping, which the game allowed with a tiny delay that he noted. He found his first real bug at a popcorn cart, where the popcorn hovered an inch above the bottom of its glass case, and felt the pleasure he always felt at a first bug, a small hard click like a key turning. The world was made. Things that are made have seams.

He was writing the popcorn bug, speaking it aloud for the headset's built-in recorder, when a voice behind him said:

"Ooh, you found one. You've got good eyes, friend."

David turned so fast he nearly stepped outside the tape.

The clown was standing about ten feet away.

Close up, in that long golden light, he was magnificent. David could think of no other word. He was tall, taller than David, made taller by a pair of oversized shoes with turned-up toes, cherry red. His suit was divided down the middle, one side a deep plum and the other a mustard yellow, with large white buttons running down the seam like a row of teeth. The ruffled collar was enormous, layered, faintly yellowed, the way old lace yellows. On his head sat a small, battered top hat at a jaunty angle, and from beneath it sprang hair the color of rust.

His face was painted white. Not the bright, smooth white of a modern costume-shop clown but something older and chalkier, faintly cracked at the edges like dried riverbed. The mouth was painted on in red, very wide, wider than the real mouth beneath it, so that his smile went on past where a smile should stop. Over each eye was a painted blue diamond. His nose was red and round and very shiny.

And the suit had pockets. That was what David noticed last, and then could not stop noticing. Pockets everywhere. Breast pockets, hip pockets, pockets on the sleeves and down the legs, pockets on top of pockets, each of them puffed and lumpy with whatever was inside.

"Welcome, welcome," the clown said, and swept off the top hat and bowed so low the brim brushed the gravel. "Welcome to Bramble Kingdom, the wildest little kingdom in the whole Ohio Valley. My name is Mister Pocket, and I'll be your guide today."

The voice was warm. That was the worst of it, though David didn't know yet that it was the worst. It was a lovely voice, rich and friendly, with a hint of an accent he couldn't place, from nowhere and everywhere, the voice of a children's television host, the voice of somebody's favorite uncle. The animation was flawless. When Mister Pocket straightened up and put his hat back on, the hat settled with a small realistic wobble. When he smiled, the painted cracks at the corners of his mouth deepened.

David found his own voice.

"Hello," he said. Then, because he was a tester and a tester tests: "Are you a scripted character?"

Mister Pocket laughed. It was a good laugh, low and delighted. "Am I scripted? Friend, I've been doing this routine for so long, I could do it in my sleep. I do do it in my sleep. Now. Where would you like to go first? The flume? The carousel? I see you've already had a ride on Zelda. She's a good old girl, our Zelda. Bites a little."

"Zelda."

"The zebra." The clown winked. One painted blue diamond folded neatly in half. "Come on. Let me show you around."

And he turned and began walking down the midway, his big red shoes slapping the gravel, and after a moment David followed, because that was the job, wasn't it? Follow the guide. See what breaks.

✦

What broke, over the next hour, was nothing.

That was what David would think about later, that night and on many nights after. Mister Pocket showed him the whole midway. Ring toss, milk-bottle pyramid, a shooting gallery of tin monkeys. He showed him the log flume, its chute running with water that looked real enough to drink, despite the build notes' warning that the shader was incomplete. He showed him a snack bar called the Banana Hut and a souvenir shop called Kingdom Treasures, where shelves of plastic animals and snow globes and pennants glittered in the light from the windows. At every stop, the clown had a joke, a bit of patter, a little history.

"Opened in nineteen seventy-four, the summer of the big floods," he said by the flume. "They said it couldn't be done, building a jungle in Ohio. They said the frost would kill the vines. Well." He gestured grandly at the green that spilled over every fence and wall. "Some things are hard to kill."

He was charming, attentive, and funny. He asked David questions and listened to the answers. He tripped over his shoes on purpose, twice, and once pulled a bunch of paper flowers out of his sleeve. The animation never stuttered. The voice never looped. When David tried, as testers do, to break him, by walking away mid-sentence, crouching, speaking nonsense, or asking him what year it was, Mister Pocket simply adapted. He followed. He crouched down too, with a theatrical groan for his knees. He answered nonsense with nonsense. When asked the year, he said, "Why, it's always summer in the Kingdom," which was a good, evasive, in-character answer, the kind a skilled writer would give a character to hide the edges of his programming.

That was the thing David kept circling back to. Edges. A scripted character had edges. You could find the places where the writing ran out and the character repeated himself, or said I don't understand, or stood blankly waiting for the right input. Every character in every game David had ever tested had those edges. He found them the way a blind man finds a wall.

Mister Pocket had no edges at all.

Near the end of the hour, they stood at the bottom of the hill below the gorilla house. Up close, the dome was bigger than David remembered, the green glass panes clouded with age and grime, a few cracked. A path led up to it, roped off with a red velvet cord on brass stands. A hand-lettered sign hung from the cord: CLOSED FOR RENOVATION.

"What about that?" David said.

The clown's smile didn't move. Clowns' smiles never moved; they were painted on. But something behind the smile seemed to settle, the way still water settles over a dropped stone.

"Oh, you don't want to go up there, friend," Mister Pocket said. "The apes are in a mood. They get in moods. Big, nasty, jealous things. They'll pull your arms right off and beat you with the wet ends." He chuckled. "That's a joke. Mostly. No, no. The apes are not for us. Let's go back down and get you a lemonade."

"In the build notes, it says the gorilla house is closed."

"Then it must be true," said Mister Pocket.

Somewhere up in the green dome, something moved behind the clouded glass. A darkness passing across darkness. Big.

David's timer went off on the phone in his pocket. In the headset, he heard it as a faint muffled buzzing, as if from another room.

"That's my time," he said. "I have to go."

"So soon?" The clown put a gloved hand to his chest, wounded. "But we were just getting acquainted. Tell me something before you go, friend. Just to be sociable."

"Sure."

"Who else lives in your house?"

The question was asked in exactly the same warm, easy tone as every other question. Would you like a lemonade? Have you ever been on a log flume before? It took David a full second to understand what he'd been asked, and in that second, he found that he had begun to answer it. His mouth had actually opened. My son, Logan. It was right there.

He shut his mouth.

"That's a strange question for a park guide," he said.

"Is it?" Mister Pocket tilted his head. The painted smile stretched wide. "I only like to know who's coming. I like to have something ready. A little something in my pocket." He patted one of the lumpy pockets on his chest. Something inside it shifted. "For each and every guest."

David took off the headset.

✦

He sat on the floor of the dining room for a long time. Then he picked up his laptop and wrote, in the flat language he trusted:

Session 2. Duration 1h 31m. Character "Mister Pocket" present throughout, contrary to build notes ("No characters in this build"). Character dialogue is unusually adaptive; I was unable to find any limit to his responses. Possible integration of an unannounced conversational AI system? If so, it is very sophisticated. Note: this character asks the player for personal information about other members of the household. I would strongly recommend removing that line. Severity: HIGH.

He looked at that for a while. Then he added, beneath it, a line he didn't usually include in bug reports, because it wasn't a bug:

I don't like him.

He emailed the file to the only address he had for Mott Interactive, which was printed in small letters at the bottom of the letter. It was a strange old address at a free provider that had been dead for years, the kind that people used in 1998, and he was certain it would bounce.

It didn't bounce. And just before three-thirty, when the school bus sighed to a stop at the corner and Logan's hood appeared at the bottom of Bell Street, an answer arrived.

It said only: Thank you. That is exactly the kind of thing I need you to tell me. Please keep the headset where your son cannot reach it. —W.M.

David read it twice. Then he looked up at the high shelf in the dining room, where the headset sat beside the photograph of Nora.

He hadn't mentioned a son. Not in the email. Not to Mott. Not to anyone.

Chapter Three

Hattie

Halloway Primate Sanctuary sat at the end of a gravel road eleven miles north of Vessey, on two hundred acres of old farmland that a dairy heiress named Ruth Halloway had left in trust in 1981 "for the care of creatures who have been wronged by people." That was how the plaque by the gate put it. Nora had loved that plaque. She used to touch it every morning on her way in, the way some people touch a doorframe for luck.

David hadn't been out in eight months. Maybe nine. He'd meant to come. Rosa Delgado had texted him on Logan's birthday, at Christmas, and once in the spring with a photograph of a newborn capuchin who'd been named Nora, which David had looked at for a long time and not answered.

But on Saturday morning Logan came downstairs already dressed, which never happened, and said, "Can we go see Hattie?" And David, who had slept badly, who had dreamed of a red velvet rope and something dark moving behind green glass, said yes before he'd thought about it at all.

They drove with the windows cracked. The October air smelled of wet leaves and woodsmoke. The trees along the road had turned, maples in flame and sumac in rust, and the sky was the clean hard blue that comes after three days of rain. Logan rode with his forehead against the passenger window and didn't put his headphones on. David noticed that, and didn't say anything, in case saying something would make it stop.

"Why Hattie?" he said instead, near the gate. "Today, I mean."

Logan shrugged one shoulder. "I don't know. I was thinking about her."

"About the gorilla house? At Bramble Kingdom?"

A pause. "Maybe."

"You didn't go in my headset, did you?"

"No." Too fast? David couldn't tell. Logan turned to look at him, and his gray-green eyes were steady. "You put it on the shelf. I can't reach the shelf."

That was almost true. Logan could reach the shelf if he stood on a chair. But David had looked at the headset that morning, and it had been exactly where he'd left it, angled exactly as he'd left it, and its little amber light had been dark.

✦

Rosa met them at the visitor center. She was a short, square woman in her fifties with silver hair cut close to her skull and a face weathered to leather by thirty years of outdoor work. She wore a fleece vest covered in mud and a lanyard heavy with keys. When she saw Logan she opened her arms, and Logan, who did not hug anyone anymore, who stiffened like a board when his aunt Claire tried it at Thanksgiving, walked straight into them.

"Look at you," Rosa said into the top of his head. "You got tall. You got so tall, mijo. Who said you could do that?"

"Nobody," Logan said, muffled.

Over his head, Rosa looked at David. It wasn't an accusing look. That was almost worse. It was just a look that said you've been gone a long time, and I know, and I'm not going to say anything about it today.

"David."

"Rosa. Hi. Sorry, it's been..."

"It's been." She let Logan go and clapped him on the shoulder. "Come on. She's been in a funny mood all week. Maybe you'll cheer her up."

✦

The gorilla habitat was at the back of the property, past the capuchin island and the long low barn where the chimpanzees lived. The building itself was poured concrete and steel, painted a soft green, unglamorous. Halloway didn't put on shows. But behind it, through a wall of thick laminated glass, lay two acres of grass and hillocks and tall oaks, with climbing frames made of telephone poles and fire hose, and a stream that ran down through the middle of it all into a shallow pond.

Halloway had five gorillas. Four of them were younger, rescues from a roadside zoo in Tennessee that had closed in 2014. The fifth was Hattie.

She was sitting by herself under the biggest of the oaks, on a nest of straw she'd pulled together, her back against the trunk. Even from a distance David could see she was old. Gorillas went gray when they aged, not just silverbacks; it came in at the muzzle and the brow and spread, and Hattie was gray nearly everywhere now, a soft ashy pelt like the coat of a very old dog. Her body had grown heavy and slow. Her hands lay in her lap, one cupped over the other, like an old woman waiting for a bus.

"Fifty-two," Rosa said quietly, as they came up to the glass. "Oldest female in North America outside of a zoo, last we checked. Arthritis in both knees, early heart disease. We've got her on a beta blocker, hidden in kiwi. She's still sharp, though. Sharp as anything."

"Does she still sign?" Logan asked.

"Every day. Not as much as she used to. She's got a lot less to say to us, I think." Rosa smiled. "Or she's said it all and we were too slow to listen."

David knew the story, or the outline of it. Hattie had come to Halloway in the winter of 1989 as a juvenile, half-starved, with burns on her back and her left hand. She'd arrived already knowing some sign language: thirty or forty words, crude, idiosyncratic, taught by someone at the place she came from. Halloway's founding director had been a linguist before she was a primatologist, and she had built on what Hattie knew until Hattie had nearly two hundred signs. Hattie had been on the cover of a science magazine in the nineties. There was a documentary. Nora, at nineteen, had watched it in a college dorm room and decided in a single evening what she was going to do with her life.

What David hadn't known, until Logan said it in the car, was where Hattie had come from before that.

"Rosa," he said. "Did she come from Bramble Kingdom?"

Rosa turned to look at him. For a moment her face was very still.

"Where'd you hear that?"

"Logan said Nora told him."

"Nora told him." Rosa breathed out through her nose. "Yeah. Yeah, she did. That's not something we put on the website. The park went bankrupt in eighty-nine, there was the fire... she was the only animal that got out. The state took her. Ruth Halloway took her from the state." She glanced at Logan, then back at David. "Why?"

David opened his mouth to tell her about the headset, about the contract, about Mister Pocket and the closed gorilla house and the question that had been waiting under the clown's warm voice like a hook under bait. He found he didn't know how to begin.

"Just curious," he said.

Rosa looked at him for another long second and said nothing.

Logan had gone up to the glass. He put his palm flat against it, the way his mother had taught him: not tapping, never tapping, just a hand at rest. Under the oak, Hattie raised her head.

She looked at him for a while across the grass. Then, very slowly, with a lot of effort, she got up.

"Huh," said Rosa softly.

It took Hattie almost two minutes to cross the habitat. She came on all fours, the way gorillas walk, on the knuckles of her hands, but she stopped twice to rest, and the second time she sat down in the grass for a while and looked at nothing. A younger gorilla, a big male named Kito, ambled over to investigate, and Hattie gave him a short, low grunt without looking at him, and he went away again.

When she reached the glass, she sat down in front of Logan. Her face was a few inches from his hand. Up close, the gray of her muzzle was threaded with white. Her eyes were the dark, liquid brown of old coffee, rimmed with heavy creases. Her breath fogged the glass in small slow puffs.

She did not look at Logan's eyes. She looked at his hand, his shoulder, the space beside his head. Gorillas did that. Nora had explained it to Logan when he was four, crouched in front of this same glass: A stare means you want to fight, baby. So we look a little to the side. That's polite. That's how you say, I'm a friend.

Logan looked a little to the side. Hattie made a low, soft sound in her chest, a rumbling purr, a sound like a contented engine.

"That's a greeting," Rosa whispered. "That's a hello. She hasn't done that for anybody new in years."

"I'm not new," Logan said, without turning. "I'm Nora's."

Something happened in David's throat.

Hattie raised one of her hands. The left one, the one that had been burned; the skin across its back was ridged and pale, and two of the fingers didn't fully straighten. She held it up between her face and the glass, and with great deliberation, she made a sign.

Rosa straightened.

"What was that?" David said.

"MOTHER." Rosa's voice was careful. "She's saying mother. She used to... with Nora... that was her name sign for Nora. Mother. She always called Nora that."

Hattie made the sign again. Then a second sign, slower: a flat hand drawn down across her own face.

"GONE," Rosa said. "Mother gone."

"I know," said Logan. His voice was small. "I know she is."

Hattie sat back. She looked at the space beside his head for a long time. The younger gorillas had drifted closer and were watching from a respectful distance: Kito, and a female named Dot, and the two little ones, Bean and Pip, who sat close together and stared at the human boy with frank curiosity.

Then Hattie's hands began to move again, faster.

The first sign was a curve drawn beside her mouth, a finger hooking up at the corner and outward, past where the corner should be. The second was both hands opening outward from her chest, fingers splayed. The third was a fist tapped twice against her own brow.

The sounds of the habitat seemed to drain away. Even the young gorillas had gone still.

Rosa didn't translate. David turned to her. She was staring at Hattie with her mouth slightly open.

"Rosa?"

"She hasn't made that sign in... I don't even know. Fifteen years. Twenty." Rosa licked her lips. "It's not one we taught her. It's one of the ones she came with. We never figured out exactly what it meant."

"What do you think it means?"

Rosa looked at Logan, at his hand against the glass, at the old gorilla on the other side with her ruined fingers raised.

"The first one's SMILE," she said slowly. "But wrong. Too big. We used to write it down as BAD SMILE. The second's MAN. And the third... the third she only ever made when she was frightened. When there was a storm, or the generator failed, or once when a new keeper wore a Halloween mask to the staff party and she saw it through the window. Nora thought it meant HIDE."

Hattie made the signs a third time. Bad smile. Man. Hide. Then she reached out and put her own palm flat against the glass, directly over Logan's.

Logan didn't move. He didn't look at Hattie's face. He looked a little to the side, the way his mother had taught him, and his shoulders were shaking very slightly.

✦

They were quiet on the drive home. The light was going gold over the fields, the same long gold light, David noticed with an unpleasant jolt, as the light that hung forever over the parking lot of Bramble Kingdom.

"Dad," Logan said, when they turned onto Route 9 heading south. The road ran past the reservoir here, and beyond it, eight miles on, the ruins. You couldn't see them from the highway. There was only a turnoff, chained, and a sign so faded it was nearly blank.

"Yeah?"

"Do you think animals remember things? Like, really remember? For a really long time?"

David kept his eyes on the road.

"Your mom thought so," he said. "Your mom thought Hattie remembered everything."

Logan nodded. He was turning something over in his hands, something small he'd had in his jacket pocket. David glanced at it and saw it was a little paper ticket, faded orange, the kind that came on a roll. Logan saw him looking and closed his hand over it.

"What's that?"

"Nothing," Logan said. "It was just in my pocket." He put it away. "I don't know where I got it."

They drove on past the chained turnoff, and neither of them looked at it, and neither of them said anything at all.

Chapter Four

Night Build

Logan Kerrigan had a rule about the stairs, and the rule was: third step, fifth step, ninth step, skip.

He'd worked it out the winter after Mom died, when he couldn't sleep, which was most nights. The third step groaned like an old man sitting down. The fifth made a bright little crack. The ninth, close to the top, let out a long, low whine. If you skipped those three and kept your weight on the wall side, you could get from the upstairs hallway to the kitchen in total silence, and Dad would never know you'd gone down to stand in front of the open fridge at two in the morning, or sit on the back steps in your coat looking at the dark yard, or watch videos of Mom on the old laptop in the den with the volume turned all the way down.

He didn't do that last one so much anymore. It had stopped helping. It had started being the opposite of helping.

On Saturday night, at twelve minutes past one, he went down the stairs. Third, fifth, ninth, skip. He stood in the dark kitchen and listened. The furnace ticked. The refrigerator hummed. From the den came the faint sound of the TV, which meant Dad had fallen asleep on the couch again with some cooking show still murmuring away. He did that a lot. Logan didn't think his dad really slept in his bed at all. Not since.

The dining room was dark. The two monitors were black. Logan pulled out Dad's desk chair very slowly, so the wheels wouldn't squeak, and climbed onto it.

The headset was cold in his hands. He'd known it would be. He didn't know how he'd known.

He'd lied to Dad in the car, on the way to Halloway. Not about tonight; tonight hadn't happened yet. But about the ticket. He knew where he'd gotten the ticket. He hadn't gone anywhere to get it, which was why it was so strange. But he'd dreamed, on Thursday night, about standing at the gate of a park with a gorilla carved over the entrance, and a man in a booth handing him a ticket and saying Admit one. When he woke up on Friday morning, the ticket had been in the pocket of his pajamas.

He'd put it in his jacket and carried it all day at school. He'd kept taking it out and looking at it. BRAMBLE KINGDOM, ADMIT ONE, 1987, in blurry red ink. It was real. The paper was soft, the way very old paper is soft. It smelled like an attic.

He'd thought he would be scared. Instead, mostly, he felt chosen.

✦

He carried the headset up to his room, because the dining room was too close to the den. Third, fifth, ninth. He closed his door all the way, pushed his desk chair against it in case Dad woke up, and then he sat down cross-legged on the floor between his bed and the window and put the headset on.

Darkness. Crickets.

The gate.

It was night in Bramble Kingdom.

He hadn't expected that. In his dream it had been afternoon. But this was full night, a big soft summer night with a sky full of more stars than he had ever seen from Vessey, and a moon hanging low and orange over the tops of the fake jungle trees. The park was lit up. Every building, every fence and tower was edged in strings of colored bulbs, red and yellow and green, and the arch over the gate was a blaze of light. The carved gorilla in its center was lit from below, its closed wooden eyes deep in shadow.

The parking lot was empty. No cars at all. Just Logan, alone in the gravel, in a pool of yellow from a single tall lamp.

"Hello?" he said.

From inside the gates, the calliope music started up. A little tune, jaunty and simple, five notes going up and five notes coming down. Logan had the strangest feeling he'd heard it before. He took a step toward the gate.

"There he is!"

Logan almost tore the headset off. The voice came from right beside him, warm and delighted, and when he spun around, there was a man there. A clown. Tall, with a little top hat and red shoes and a suit that was purple on one side and yellow on the other, and a face as white as a plate, and a mouth painted so wide it reached almost to his ears.

The clown was down on one knee, so his face was level with Logan's. He had his hands clasped in front of his chest like someone receiving a present.

"Logan Kerrigan," the clown said. "As I live and breathe. I have been so looking forward to this."

Logan's heart was banging. He told himself it was a game. It was a really, really good game, but a game. "How do you know my name?"

"How do I know your name?" The clown laughed. It was a nice laugh. It sounded like being tickled by somebody you trusted. "Why, you're on the guest list, of course. Special guest. V.I.P. You came for your ticket on Thursday. Don't you remember? You've still got it, haven't you?"

Logan's hand went to the pocket of his pajama pants, in the real world. Nothing there. The ticket was in his jacket, downstairs, in the hall. But the clown nodded as if he'd seen Logan check.

"Good boy. Hang onto it. You'll need it." The clown stood up, unfolding to his full enormous height, and offered Logan one white-gloved hand. "My name is Mister Pocket. And tonight, my friend, the Kingdom is open just for you."

✦

Logan didn't take the hand. He didn't know why. He wanted to. Every part of him that liked games, liked secrets, liked being picked first for once, wanted to put his hand in that clean white glove. But some small, stubborn, cold part of him that sounded like his mother said, Not yet.

Mister Pocket didn't seem to mind. He just tucked the hand away in one of his many pockets and gestured with the other toward the gate. "After you, then."

Logan walked into Bramble Kingdom.

It was the most beautiful place he had ever seen. He'd been in VR worlds before: space stations, castles, the bottom of the sea. He'd climbed mountains and flown dragons. None of it was like this. None of it felt like this. The air seemed to have a smell, of popcorn and cotton candy and cut grass and something green and wet underneath. The fountain with the three stone elephants was lit up from inside, and the water came out of their trunks in glowing arcs, gold and pink. The carousel was turning all by itself with nobody on it, its jungle animals rising and falling to the calliope tune: the zebra, a tiger, a lion, a rhinoceros, a giraffe whose head stuck up through the roof.

"Want to ride?" said Mister Pocket. "Anything you like. No lines tonight."

"Maybe," Logan said. "Later."

"Later," agreed the clown. "We've got all the later in the world."

They walked the midway. The game booths were lit and open, but the people behind the counters were strange, pale, fuzzy shapes that faded when he got close, like breath on cold glass. Mister Pocket didn't seem to see them. At the ring toss, he put a ring in Logan's hand. Logan felt it, actually felt it, the weight of it in the controller, though he knew that was impossible, just a buzz of haptics, just his brain filling in. He threw it, and it landed perfectly over the neck of a bottle, and a bell rang, and the pale shape behind the counter held out a prize: a small plush gorilla with button eyes.

Mister Pocket's hand closed over it first.

"Oh, not that one," he said lightly. "Not that one, no, no. Ugly old thing. Have this instead." And he reached into a pocket on his sleeve and pulled out a little red rubber ball, which he pressed into Logan's palm. "There. Much better. A nose, just like mine. For when you join the show."

Logan looked at the ball in his hand. He looked at the clown's face, so close now, bent over him. The paint was cracked at the edges, like a dry riverbed. Under the paint, in the cracks, there was something darker than skin.

"What show?" he said.

"The big show. The best show. Every night, under the Big Top." Mister Pocket waved a hand toward the far end of the park, where a striped tent rose against the stars, its peak lit by a single searchlight swinging slowly back and forth. "Everybody comes. Everybody. You'll love it, I promise. I've got a seat saved for you right in the front row."

"Can my dad come?"

There was a tiny pause. It was so small that Logan wasn't sure afterward that he'd really heard it.

"Your dad." Mister Pocket put his hands on his knees and leaned down until his huge painted smile was right in front of Logan's face. "Does your dad even know you're here, Logan?"

Logan didn't answer.

"I didn't think so." The clown's voice was gentle, conspiratorial. "That's all right. That's just fine. Dads don't always understand. Dads get busy. Dads have their screens and their work and their sad, sad faces, and they don't want to play anymore. Isn't that right?"

"I guess."

"But you and me, we know how to play." Mister Pocket straightened. "Come on. There's something you'll want to see."

✦

He led Logan past the midway, past the Banana Hut and the shooting gallery and the long dark chute of the log flume, to the bottom of a small hill. At the top of the hill, a great dome of green glass rose against the sky. It was dark. Every other building in the park was lit, but the dome was dark, and a red velvet rope stretched across the path, with a sign on it that said CLOSED FOR RENOVATION.

Logan knew what it was. He'd known as soon as he saw it from the gate.

"The gorilla house," he said.

Mister Pocket made a sour face. It was a very funny face, the kind a cartoon makes when it bites a lemon, and Logan almost laughed. "The ape house," the clown corrected him. "And it's closed, see? Closed, closed, closed. Not that you'd want to go in. Nasty, smelly brutes. Violent. Sneaky. You can't trust an ape, Logan. They'll look at you like they're your friend, and then..." He made a sudden, savage tearing motion with his gloved hands, as if ripping a piece of paper in half. "Never go near the apes. Promise me."

Logan stared up at the dome. Behind the clouded glass, something moved.

It was huge. A darkness against darkness, a shape that blotted out the faint reflected glow of the park lights as it passed. It moved slowly, heavily, from one side of the dome to the other. Then it stopped.

And Logan understood, with a cold certainty that went all the way down to the soles of his feet on his bedroom carpet, that it was looking at him.

He didn't look back. Some instinct he didn't know he had made him drop his eyes, look a little to the side, at the grass of the hill, at the corner of the dome. That's polite. That's how you say, I'm a friend.

"Logan?" Mister Pocket's voice, close to his ear, a little sharper than before. "You haven't promised."

"I promise," Logan heard himself say.

"Good boy." The clown's hand settled on his shoulder. Logan felt it, felt the weight, though there was no weight. The controllers buzzed faintly. "That's my good boy. Now let's go back, and have a lemonade, and you can tell me all about yourself. I want to hear everything. Everything about your dad. Everything about your house. And everything," the warm voice dropped, soft as velvet, "about your mother."

Behind the green glass, the huge dark shape turned around.

It turned its back. Logan saw it go: the vast hunched shoulders, the slow deliberate pivot. It stood with its back to the clown, and to the boy, and to the whole bright humming park, like someone refusing to watch a thing they could not stop.

✦

When Logan took off the headset, it was four-forty in the morning. He had been inside for three and a half hours. It had felt like forty minutes.

He crept back down the stairs. Third, fifth, ninth, skip. He climbed onto Dad's desk chair and put the headset back on the shelf, exactly how it had been, beside the photograph of Mom laughing with a baby gorilla's fingers wrapped around her own. Then he went back upstairs and got into bed, and lay shivering under the covers until the windows went gray.

When he finally fell asleep, his hand was closed tight around something small and round and rubbery. In the morning, he found it in the bed with him.

A red rubber ball. A clown's nose.

He put it in the bottom drawer of his desk, under his old Lego instructions, and he didn't tell anyone. Not that day. Not for a long time.

Chapter Five

Patch Notes

The play log was David's own idea, and he would come to wish he'd never had it.

On Sunday afternoon, with Logan upstairs doing homework, or claiming to, David took the headset down from the shelf, intending to begin his third session. He'd been thinking about Mister Pocket for two days in the low persistent way you think about a toothache, and he'd decided the best approach was the professional one. He would go in with a plan. He would map every boundary of the clown's conversation, document it, and send the findings to Mott in a report so dry and complete that the whole thing would become, once and for all, a technical problem.

First, though, he wanted the logs. Every build he had ever tested kept some kind of record: session start, session end, crashes, frame-rate drops. They were usually hidden in a developer menu. He spent twenty minutes inside the system panel, pressing buttons in combinations testers learn the way locksmiths learn the feel of pins, and finally, by holding the system button and squeezing both triggers at once, he made a second panel slide into view beneath the first.

It was plain green text on black, like an old terminal.

S1  TUE 16:52  -  17:06   KERRIGAN, D.
S2  WED 09:02  -  10:33   KERRIGAN, D.
S3  SAT 01:12  -  04:41   GUEST

He went upstairs.

He didn't handle it well. He knew that while it was happening, and he couldn't stop it, the way you can't stop a car on ice.

He opened Logan's door without knocking. Logan was on his bed with his math book open and his phone in his hand. He looked up, startled, then saw the headset in David's hand, and his face did something that told David everything.

"Don't. There's a log, Logan. It's all logged. One in the morning until almost five. I told you. I told you it wasn't for kids, it says it right in the letter, I told you to stay away from it, and you climbed up on my chair in the middle of the night like a... like a..."

W.M.

He reads what I write.

He spent most of that night on the internet, because that was the kind of thing David knew how to do.

Bramble Kingdom didn't have much of a presence online. It had closed before the web was born, and it hadn't been important enough to anyone outside Vessey to earn more than a few paragraphs in the corners of local-history forums. But there were scraps, and David had spent fifteen years finding scraps. He found a fan page last updated in 2009, a single long scrolling column of scanned postcards and brochure pages. He found a newspaper archive with a paywall that he paid. He found a forum thread titled Anyone else remember BK? (Vessey OH) with a hundred and twelve replies.

He read about the opening day in 1974, when the county commissioner cut a ribbon of fake jungle vines. He read about the log flume, the carousel, the famous Banana Split Challenge at the Banana Hut. He read about the gorilla house, which had opened in 1979 as the park's crown jewel, "the only great-ape exhibit between Cincinnati and Pittsburgh." Six gorillas, purchased from a private collector in Florida. The forum posters remembered them with that strange tenderness people have for animals they saw once as children. The big silverback was named Bosun, one wrote. He used to sit with his back to the glass and ignore everybody, it was hilarious. Another: There was a baby one, they used to bring her out for the show. Hattie? Harriet?

The show. David scrolled up and down the thread, piecing it together.

The Pocket Revue had run twice daily in the Big Top from 1979 to 1988. It starred a clown called Mister Pocket and "his jungle friends," the gorillas, performing tricks. They rode tricycles. They wore little hats. They did a routine in which Mister Pocket pretended to be afraid of them and they chased him around the ring, and the children screamed with laughter. He was SO funny, a woman posted. I still remember the bit where he'd pull all that stuff out of his pockets. Endless. Hankies, flowers, a rubber chicken. And then he'd come out into the crowd and say "Want to see what's in my pocket?" and you'd say yes and he'd pull out something of YOURS. Like your hair ribbon or your mom's car keys. I never figured out how he did it.

Beneath that, someone had written: I hated that clown.

And beneath that, a dozen replies, from a dozen strangers who had been children in Vessey in the 1980s. Me too. He scared me to death. My little brother had nightmares for a year. Something about his eyes. I remember my dad saying the man who played him was the gorilla trainer too and he was mean to them, my dad worked at the feed store and they bought a lot of something called "corrective supplies." He was. David found it in the newspaper archive, in the front page of the Vessey Courier for October 30, 1989.

FIRE DESTROYS BRAMBLE KINGDOM Park Employee Feared Dead; Animals Lost

The fire had begun shortly after midnight, the article said, in or near the Big Top, and had spread across the park on a high wind. By the time fire crews arrived from three counties, the tent, the gorilla house, and most of the midway were gone. Five of the six gorillas had died. A juvenile female had been found at dawn sheltering in a culvert near the reservoir, badly burned but alive. And one employee had been inside the park at the time of the fire and was presumed dead: Cyril Mott, 49, the park's head animal trainer, who performed for years as the clown Mister Pocket.

The park had been scheduled to close permanently the following week. It had been bankrupt for a year.

Mott. David sat back in his chair. The name went through him like cold water. W. Mott. It wasn't a coincidence. It couldn't be.

He kept reading, and found something else. It was buried deeper in the archive, a year earlier, a series of short articles from the summer of 1988 that ran on pages four and six, the way news about other people's grief runs in a small town.

LOCAL GIRL FOUND SAFE AFTER PARK SCARE. A seven-year-old girl separated from her family at Bramble Kingdom had been found three hours later, unharmed, sitting on a bench outside the funhouse. She had no memory of where she'd been.

SECOND CHILD WANDERS AT PARK. An eight-year-old boy, same story. Found near the Big Top at closing time. Unharmed. No memory.

And then, in late August, SEARCH CONTINUES FOR MISSING VESSEY BOY. Daniel Pruitt, nine, had last been seen near the gorilla house at about four o'clock on a Saturday afternoon. He had been wearing a red-and-white striped shirt and blue shorts. Volunteers had searched the park and the woods around the reservoir for six days. His family asked anyone with information to call.

There was a photograph. A school portrait, grainy in the scan: a skinny kid with a gap in his front teeth and a cowlick standing straight up at the crown of his head, grinning with that reckless, open delight of children who have not yet learned to be careful of cameras.

He was never found.

Logan's eyes dropped. He did it without thinking, the way you jerk your hand from a hot stove. He looked at the black water of the pool. At the rocks. At the space beside the gorilla's head. That's polite. That's how you say, I'm a friend.

Bosun stood up.

He rose onto his legs, all the way up, and he was enormous, he was a wall, he was a black shape against the moonlit glass that blotted out half the dome. He opened his mouth and roared. Then Bosun beat his chest with both cupped hands, a hollow drumming pok-pok-pok-pok-pok that echoed off the glass like gunshots, and he dropped to all fours and charged.

He came straight across the pool. Black water exploded in sheets of silver. He stood frozen at the end of the path with his eyes locked on the ground a few feet to the side of the charging silverback, every muscle in his body screaming. He didn't look up. He didn't look up. He did not look up.

Never run, baby. If a silverback charges, you don't run, and you don't look. You make yourself small and you look down and you wait. It's a test. He just wants to know you're not a threat.

Bosun stopped a yard away.

The ground shook. Leaves rained down. The great dark bulk loomed over Logan, breathing hard, hot breath that Logan could somehow feel on the top of his head. He stayed small. He stayed looking down. His whole body was trembling. He could hear himself making a high thin sound in his throat, like a kettle.

For a long moment, nothing happened.

Then, very slowly, Bosun turned around.

He turned his back on Logan. He sat down, deliberately, heavily, on the earth in front of him, with his vast silver back turned, and he stayed there. Not looking. Not moving. A mountain of frost in the moonlight, sitting with its back to a boy.

Logan let out his breath in a shaky gasp.

And then he saw that they weren't alone.

The others had been there all along, at the edges of the clearing, in the shadows under the trees. A big female with a wide, solemn face, older, gray at the muzzle; somehow Logan knew her name was Mabel. Two more females, younger. A smaller male, half-grown, all gangly arms and curiosity, crouched on a low branch: Tuck. And beside Tuck, holding the young gorilla's hand like a child holding a friend's hand at a crosswalk, was a boy.

A boy in a red-and-white striped shirt, and blue shorts, with a cowlick sticking straight up from the crown of his head.

He looked about nine. He looked like he'd been nine forever.

As Logan watched, every one of them turned around. Mabel. The young females. Tuck. The boy. One by one, they turned their backs on him, the way Bosun had, and sat down in the moonlight, silent and still.

And Logan understood that it wasn't rejection. It wasn't rudeness. It was a lesson. They were showing him. This is how. This is how you do it. This is the thing he can't stand.

"I don't understand," Logan whispered. "Please. I don't..."

The boy in the striped shirt spoke without turning his head. His voice was very soft, and very old.

"Don't look at him," he said. "He's only real when you look."

Then, from the far side of the dome, where the path ran out through the doors and down the hill into the park, a voice called Logan's name.

Not the clown's voice.

"Logan? Baby? There you are."

He turned. He couldn't help it. He turned toward the doors, toward the colored lights below the hill, and there, standing on the path in the moonlight with her hand held out to him, wearing her muddy Halloway fleece and her lanyard heavy with keys, with her hair tied back the way she'd always tied it, and her gray-green eyes, and her smile, exactly her smile...

"Mom," Logan said.

Behind him, the gorillas made no sound at all.

Chapter Nine

Nora

A clearing of moonlit grass on a hillside, with colored lights glowing in the distance. A path. And on the path, a few feet away, close enough to touch, a woman in a muddy fleece vest with a lanyard of keys around her neck.

She was smiling, and her eyes were gray-green, and her hair was tied back the way she'd always tied it, with a few strands escaping at the temples. She had a small scar at the edge of her chin from falling off a bike when she was ten. She had freckles on her nose that came out in summer. She was standing with her weight on one hip and her head tilted, listening, in exactly the way that Logan tilted his.

It was Nora.

It was not a vague figure, or an avatar made from guesswork. It was Nora, perfect in every detail, moving like her, breathing like her. As David watched, she laughed at something Logan said and brushed one of the loose strands of hair away from her face with the back of her wrist. He had seen her do that ten thousand times. He had watched her do it across the kitchen table. He had watched her do it on their wedding day, under a tree, in a wind.

The room went away.

He didn't fall, but it was close. He put out a hand and found the doorframe and held onto it. The air had gone out of his lungs and wouldn't come back. Somewhere far off, he could hear Logan talking, babbling, I missed you so much, I missed you every day, Dad doesn't, Dad never talks about you, and on the tablet the woman who was Nora listened and nodded and put out her hand to touch Logan's face, and Logan leaned into a touch that wasn't there.

Get it off him. The thought came from very far down, from some stubborn animal place beneath the grief. Get it off him now.

But he didn't move. He couldn't. Because she was there. Because she was on the screen, real as the day he'd lost her, and if he took the headset off his son, she'd be gone again. He'd be the one making her go. Some broken and desperate part of him whispered, Just a minute. Just one more minute. Let him have this. Let me have this.

On the tablet, Nora turned her head.

She looked straight out of the screen. Not at Logan. Through the eyes of the headset, past him, at the place where David would be standing if he were there in the moonlit grass with them. She looked right at him.

She smiled.

"Hello, David," she said.

Her voice. Her voice, God, her voice. Low, a little husky, with that faint Kentucky curl she'd never quite lost.

"I've been waiting so long for you to come and play," she said.

She lifted one hand, and waved. Slowly. Back and forth. Back and forth.

David knew that wave.

It was not Nora's wave. Nora waved like a windmill, both arms over her head, the way Logan used to wave from the corner. This was a slow, patient side-to-side, the wave of something that had been waving for a very long time and could wait a great deal longer. He'd seen it at the end of a long golden midway on a Tuesday afternoon in October. He'd seen it in a dream, an inch from his face.

The spell broke. He crossed the room in two strides.

"Logan. Logan, take it off."

"No! Dad, no, it's Mom, look, you can see her, look..."

"That's not your mom."

"It is..."

"Logan, that is not your mother." He had Logan by the shoulders. Logan twisted away, flailing, his controllers swinging, and one of them caught David across the cheekbone with a crack that made his eyes water. On the tablet, the woman who looked like Nora was no longer smiling. She was watching. Her face was very still. Her eyes had gone flat, like the eyes of a doll.

Please don't take the device away suddenly.

David didn't care. He couldn't care. He got his hands on the headset's strap and pulled it up and off his son's head in one movement.

It wasn't a scream of anger. It was pain, raw and bewildered, the scream of a much younger child who's touched the stove. His whole body went rigid and then slack. His knees buckled. David caught him as he fell and they went down together onto the carpet, and for three or four seconds that lasted a year, Logan's eyes were rolled back and white in his face, his lips gray, his fingers twitching against David's chest.

"Logan. Logan. Come on, buddy. Come on. Look at me. Look at me."

Logan gasped. A huge, ragged, whooping breath, like somebody surfacing from the bottom of a pool. His eyes rolled down and found David's face. They focused.

Then they filled with something David had never seen in them before, not even on the worst nights after the funeral. It was hatred. Pure, white, absolute.

"You took her away," Logan said. His voice was a scraped whisper. "You took her away again."

He shoved David off with a strength that seemed impossible in his skinny arms and scrambled backward across the floor until his back hit the bed. He pulled his knees up and wrapped his arms around them and put his face down and began to sob, huge wrenching sobs that shook his whole body, and when David reached for him he screamed, "Don't touch me!" so loudly that a light went on in the house next door.

He could feel all of it pushing up against the back of his throat.

"Okay?"

Under the covers, something that might, in better times, have been a laugh.

Chapter Ten

Mott

The turnoff from Route 9 was chained, but the chain had been cut long ago and hung loose from one post, and a pair of tire ruts ran through the gap and away into the trees.

David sat at the mouth of the ruts with his engine running and his hands on the wheel. The morning was raw and overcast, the sky the color of dishwater, and a fine cold mist hung in the hollows of the fields. The faded sign by the turnoff had once been painted green and yellow; now it was mostly bare gray wood, and you had to know what it had said to read it. BRAMBLE KINGDOM 1 MI. FUN FOR THE WHOLE FAMILY! Somebody had spray-painted a crude grinning face across the bottom half, long enough ago that the paint had faded to pink.

He put the car in gear and drove in.

The ruts ran through second-growth woods, ash and box elder and sumac, their leaves gone blood-red and dropping. Wild grape vines hung from the branches in long curtains. Twice the car scraped its belly over a hump of buried concrete, and once he passed the rusted skeleton of something that might have been a ticket kiosk, tipped on its side in the ferns. Then the trees fell back, and he was in the parking lot.

It was enormous and empty and broken, a cracked gray plain of asphalt heaved up by roots and split by weeds. Light poles stood at intervals, most of them leaning, a few fallen, their lamps long gone. There were no cars. Of course there were no cars. But he found himself looking for them anyway, for the station wagons and the brown sedan and the yellow pickup with its camper shell, and when he didn't see them, he felt an absurd lurch, as if something had been taken away.

At the far end of the lot stood the gate.

What was left of it. One of the wooden towers had collapsed entirely and lay in a long black heap of rotted timber, furred with moss. The other still stood, leaning, wrapped in real vines now instead of painted ones. The arch had fallen. Its pieces lay in the weeds below, and David could make out a painted letter here and there, a K, part of a D, a monkey's curling tail.

He got out of the car and walked over to the wreckage, because he couldn't help it.

The carved gorilla face lay on its back in the weeds, staring up at the gray sky. Its paint was almost all gone. The long crack through its brow had widened into a split you could put your fingers in. And its eyes, the carved wooden eyes that had been closed in the simulation, that Logan had said were open the second time...

They were closed. Of course they were closed. They'd been carved closed. David crouched beside it and touched the worn wood of its lids with his fingertips, and felt foolish, and felt cold.

✦

He didn't go into the park itself. He could see enough of it from the gate. The ruin of the fountain, the three stone elephants worn to lumps. The concrete footings of the carousel, a ring of rusted bolts. Beyond them, burned stumps of buildings poking up out of a sea of sumac and young trees, and here and there a twisted spar of black metal: the flume's chute, the frame of a game booth. It was smaller than he remembered. It was smaller than the simulation. That surprised him, and then didn't. Things are always bigger in memory. Bigger, and brighter, and more golden.

Off to the right, on a little rise above the rest of the ruins, stood the skeleton of a dome.

He knew it instantly. The green glass was gone, every pane, shattered and buried decades ago. But the frame was still there, a cage of iron ribs blackened by fire and rusted brown by thirty-seven winters, standing against the gray sky like the bones of some huge animal that had died on the hill.

He looked at it for a long time. Then he followed the ruts around the edge of the lot, past the bones of the gorilla house, back into the trees.

The caretaker's house was a quarter mile on. It was a small square farmhouse, white once, with a sagging porch and a steep roof and two brick chimneys, and it was in better shape than anything else on the property. Someone had replaced the windows. Someone had patched the roof with new shingles that didn't match the old ones. A satellite dish was bolted to the eave, and a thick black cable ran from the dish down the side of the house and into a hole drilled through the wall.

And the house was humming.

David heard it before he'd shut the car door. A low, steady electric drone, like a refrigerator the size of a building. It came from inside, from every wall at once. On the side of the house he saw three big air-conditioning units, the industrial kind, all running full tilt in forty-degree weather, blowing streams of warm air out into the cold. Beyond them, under a tarp, a generator the size of a pickup truck sat on a concrete pad.

He went up the porch steps. Before he could knock, the door opened.

He was tall and terribly thin, stooped like a question mark, in a gray cardigan that hung off him as if from a hook. His hair was white and wispy and stood up in a ragged halo. His face was long and pale and creased, with deep hollows under the cheekbones, and his eyes behind a pair of wire-rimmed glasses were huge and dark and red-rimmed, the eyes of a man who hadn't slept properly in years. "It was showing him his mother."

Wendell opened his eyes and looked at him. Then he did a strange thing. He glanced back over his shoulder, into the house, the way a man glances back at a sleeping dog. And he stepped out onto the porch and pulled the door shut behind him and said, very quietly, "Not in here. Walk with me."

Chapter Eleven

Withdrawal

Claire was sitting on the edge of Logan's bed when David got home, with a damp washcloth in one hand and her phone face-down on her knee, and the look she gave him over her shoulder was one he hadn't seen since they were children. Owen

Chapter Fourteen

Danny

"You'd better come in," she said.

The kitchen was warm and yellow and smelled of onions. There was a pot of something simmering on the stove. Lorraine turned it down to low and poured him a cup of coffee he didn't want and sat down across from him at a table covered in a plastic cloth printed with cherries.

"My grandson's in the hospital," she said, before he could speak. "Mikey. He's ten. My daughter's boy. They found him Saturday night in the middle of Route 9, walking. Walking toward the park. In his socks. A trucker nearly hit him." She wrapped both hands around her own mug. "He had one of those headsets. Some kid at school gave him a code. My daughter thinks it's drugs. The police think it's one of those internet things. But I went up to see him yesterday, and he was lying there with his headset on, humming." She looked at David with eyes that were dry and hard and very tired. "And I knew the song. I've known that song since I was fourteen years old."

"Five notes up," David said. "Five down."

"Danny hummed it all that summer." She closed her eyes. "All of July and August of nineteen eighty-eight. Morning, noon, and night. He hummed it in his sleep. Mom would yell at him to stop at the dinner table and he wouldn't even know he was doing it."

"After the clown picked him."

Her eyes opened. "You know about that."

"Marguerite Hale told me."

"Marguerite." Lorraine let out a breath that was almost a laugh. "God. I haven't thought about Marguerite in years. She was the only grown-up who ever warned me. She told me, 'Keep that boy away from the Big Top, honey.' And I didn't. I couldn't. He was nine, and I was fourteen, and I was supposed to be watching him every Saturday while Mom worked a double at the hospital laundry, and I wanted to be with my friends, not babysitting." She stared into her coffee. "So I'd drop him at the gorilla house. That's where he always wanted to be anyway. He'd stand at that glass for three, four hours, just watching them. He knew all their names. Bosun and Mabel and Ruby and Pearl and Tuck and the baby. Hattie. He loved Hattie best. She'd come to the glass for him. Put her little hands up against his."

"She still does that," David said. "With my son."

Lorraine looked up sharply. "She's alive?"

"She's at Halloway. She's fifty-two."

Something moved over Lorraine's face. She got up abruptly and went out of the kitchen, and David heard her footsteps on the stairs, and a door opening overhead, and then a long silence. When she came back, she was holding a photograph.

It was a Polaroid, faded to the warm orange tones of the late eighties. A little boy in a striped shirt was pressed up against a pane of green-tinted glass, laughing so hard his eyes were squeezed shut. On the other side of the glass, a baby gorilla, tiny, maybe two years old, had her face squashed against the glass too, her lips pushed out in a comical pout, her two small black hands spread flat over the boy's. Someone had written on the white strip at the bottom in ballpoint: Danny + Hattie, June '88.

"I took that," Lorraine said. "The week before he got picked."

David looked at the photograph for a long time. He thought of an old gray gorilla with burned fingers, putting her palm against the glass over a twelve-year-old boy's hand. He thought: She's been waiting thirty-seven years to see a boy at that glass again.

✦

"He went up into the bleachers," Lorraine said. "That was how it started. The Pocket Revue. I took Danny on the Fourth of July because there were fireworks after, and the show was free that day. And Mister Pocket did his bit, the one where he goes into the crowd. 'Want to see what's in my pocket?' And he came right up the bleachers to us. Right to Danny. Like he'd known where we were sitting all along."

She was quiet for a moment.

"And he reached into his pocket and pulled out Danny's Matchbox car. A little blue Corvette. Danny had it in his own pocket when we left the house that morning. He always had it in his pocket. And he looked at that clown holding his car, and his face just... lit up. Like it was magic. Like it was the best thing that had ever happened to him."

"And after that?"

"After that, he changed." She turned her mug around and around on the cherry-print cloth. "Not all at once. He got quiet. He stopped playing with the kids on the street. He'd go up to his room and just sit there. And he'd hum. And he started saying things. 'Mister Pocket says I'm his favorite.' 'Mister Pocket says I can be in the show.' 'Mister Pocket says I can stay forever, Lorrie, and I'll never have to go home.'" Her voice tightened. "Our dad had left that spring. Mom was working all the time. Things were bad. And this thing, this clown, had found the hole in him and crawled right in."

David thought of a voice like velvet saying, Dads get busy. Dads have their screens and their work and their sad, sad faces.

"It finds the hole," he said. "That's what it does."

"Yeah." She looked at him. "What's the hole in your boy?"

"His mother died. Two years ago." He made himself say the rest. "And I wasn't there. I haven't been there. Not since."

Lorraine didn't say anything. She just nodded slowly, once, the nod of someone who'd seen a lot of holes.

"The day he disappeared," she said, "was the twentieth of August. A Saturday. I dropped him at the gorilla house at about one. I said I'd be back at four. I went to the flume with my friends and then we went to the arcade. I was late. I got back at about a quarter after. And he was gone." She stopped. Started again. "One of the keepers said she'd seen him at about three-thirty, standing at the glass like always. And then a man in a clown suit came up the hill, and Danny turned around and saw him, and... the keeper said he ran. Not toward the clown. Away from him. Around the back of the gorilla house. Where the keepers' door was. And she lost sight of him, and she didn't think anything of it, because kids ran around back there sometimes."

"And the clown?"

"Cyril Mott told the police he'd been doing a meet-and-greet on the midway at the time. Half a dozen people backed him up. They searched the whole park. They searched the gorilla house top to bottom. They searched the woods, the reservoir. They dragged the reservoir." She swallowed. "They never found anything. Not a shoe. Not a hair. Not his little blue car."

"I'm so sorry."

"After the fire, a year later, they went through the gorilla house again. What was left of it. They found the apes. Five of them." She was quiet. "And nothing else. No sign of a child. There was one cop, an older guy, he came and told my mom. He said he thought it was good news, in a way. He said, 'Maybe your boy just ran off, ma'am. Maybe he's out there somewhere.' And Mom believed that till the day she died. She left the porch light on every night for twenty-six years."

She looked out the kitchen window at the crabapple tree in the yard.

"I never believed it," she said. "I always knew he was in that park somewhere. I could feel it. Like a splinter. I used to drive out there when I was in my twenties and stand at the fence and look at the ruins of that dome and think, He's in there. He's still in there." She laughed, a short dry sound. "My therapist had a lot to say about that."

David hesitated. Then he took out Wendell's soft old notebook, the one with the maps, and he took out a page he'd copied by hand that morning from Nora's pale blue notebook, and he laid them both on the cherry tablecloth in front of her.

H: BOY. BOY HIDE. BOY WITH BOSUN.

H: BOY STAY. BOY SAFE. BOY WITH BOSUN NOW.

Lorraine read it. She read it again. Her hand went to her mouth.

"Hattie said that?"

"To my wife. Five years ago."

"Boy safe." Her voice cracked in the middle. "She said he was safe?"

"She said it over and over. She said Bosun kept him. That the clown wanted him, and Bosun wouldn't give him up." David hesitated. "And my son, Logan... he went inside the game. Into the gorilla house. And he saw a boy there, with the troop. A boy in a red-and-white striped shirt, with a cowlick."

Lorraine Pruitt put her face in her hands and wept.

David sat with her. He didn't know what else to do. The pot simmered on the stove, and the clock over the sink ticked, and outside on Orchard Street a dog barked twice and went quiet. After a long time Lorraine lifted her head and wiped her face roughly with the dish towel.

"Mikey said something," she said. "Yesterday. At the hospital. He was lying there with the headset on, and he wasn't really talking to anybody, but he said something, and I thought he was just... I thought it was the fever." She looked at David with red, swollen eyes. "He said, 'The boy in the stripey shirt told me not to look at the clown. He said if I look at the clown I'll have to stay.'"

They looked at each other across the table.

"He's still trying," Lorraine whispered. "Thirty-seven years. He's still in there trying to warn them."

✦

David drove home in the dark with the Polaroid in his breast pocket. Lorraine had pressed it into his hand at the door. "Show it to her," she'd said. "To Hattie. And if you see him... if your boy sees him again..." She hadn't finished. She'd just held onto his hand for a moment, hard, and then let go and closed the door.

He thought about holes, as he drove. About the hole in a nine-year-old boy whose father had left, and the hole in a twelve-year-old boy whose mother had died. About the hole in a skinny kid with big ears who'd drawn maps of the park in a composition book so he'd know the way out. About the hole in himself, which he'd been filling with work and screens and silence for two years, and which a thing in a clown suit had found and crawled into as easily as walking through an open door.

Have you got a minute? I want to tell you something weird.

Can it wait?

He pulled into the driveway on Bell Street at nine-forty. The house was dark except for the towel-shaded lamp in Logan's window. He sat in the car for a minute with the engine off. Then he got out, and walked around to the back of the car, because he'd been checking it every time he passed it now, the way you keep touching a sore tooth with your tongue.

The trunk was open.

Not wide open. Just unlatched, the lid lifted an inch or two, a thin black line of darkness along its edge. He stared at it. He'd locked it. He'd locked it that night after he pulled the headset off Logan, and he'd checked it every morning since, and it had been locked every single time.

He lifted the lid.

The ice scraper. The bag of rock salt. Logan's cheap cracked secondhand headset, lying on its side in the corner.

The box with the black headset was gone.

Upstairs, in the window of his son's room, the towel-shaded lamp went out.

Chapter Fifteen

The Long Night

He didn't remember the stairs. Afterward, he would try to remember them, would try to remember unlocking the front door, crossing the dark hall, taking the steps two and three at a time, and there would be nothing there. Just the car, and the open trunk, and then he was in Logan's doorway with his hand on the light switch.

Claire's note was on the kitchen table, he would find out later. Logan asleep, fever gone, 8:45. Had to run home, Jen's got a thing. Back by 10. Call the landline if you need me. She'd been gone less than an hour.

The light came on.

Logan was sitting in the middle of his bedroom floor. He was cross-legged, very straight, his hands resting loosely on his knees, palms up, the way you'd sit in the front row of a show when the lights go down. He wore the gray hoodie he'd fallen asleep in. His feet were bare.

The black headset was on his face.

Its amber light was not pulsing. It was steady and bright, a hard burning orange, like a coal in the bottom of a grate. The empty box lay on the floor beside him, its lid tossed away, its nest of shredded green paper scattered across the carpet like grass after a storm.

"Logan."

Nothing. Logan didn't move. Below the edge of the faceplate, his mouth was slightly open, and very softly, so softly David could barely hear it, he was humming. Five up. Five down.

David dropped to his knees beside him. "Logan. Buddy. Can you hear me?"

Nothing.

He put a hand on Logan's shoulder. Under the hoodie, his son's body was rigid, every muscle locked. His skin was cold, not the fever heat of the night before but cold, clammy, like something taken out of a cellar. David put two fingers against the side of his neck below his jaw, the way Claire had shown him once, years ago, at a family CPR class Nora had made them all attend. The pulse was there. Slow. Very slow. Much too slow.

"Logan. Logan. It's Dad. Come on. Come back."

He reached for the headset's strap. His fingers closed on it.

Please don't take the device away suddenly.

He froze. He remembered the scream two nights ago, the whites of Logan's eyes, the gray lips. The three or four seconds that had lasted a year. That had been his cheap little secondhand headset, a doorway the thing had borrowed. This was the black one. This was the real door.

His hand shook on the strap.

He let go and stood up and ran downstairs to the beige phone on the kitchen wall and called 911, and then he called Claire, and then he went back upstairs and sat on the floor beside his son and held his cold hand and talked to him, said anything, said his name over and over, until he heard the sirens on Bell Street.

✦

There were two paramedics, a man and a woman, and they were very good and very fast and very calm. They checked Logan's airway and his blood pressure and his oxygen. They shone a penlight under the edge of the faceplate, trying to see his pupils. They asked David a dozen questions in the space of a minute: had he taken anything, did he have any medical conditions, was he diabetic, epileptic, had he been sick?

"He had a fever yesterday," David said. "It broke. He's been... there's a thing going around. With the kids. With the headsets. Six of them are at Regional..."

The woman paramedic glanced at her partner. "The Baptiste kid," she said. "And the girl at the football field." She turned back to David. "We need to take that off him, sir. We can't assess his eyes."

"I don't think you should."

"Sir..."

"I took one off him two nights ago and he stopped breathing. For a few seconds. I don't know what this is, but I'm telling you..."

"Sir, it's a video game headset. It's a piece of plastic." The male paramedic was already reaching for it. "We'll do it slowly."

He did do it slowly. He eased his fingers under the strap at the back of Logan's head and lifted, and the faceplate came away from Logan's face by an inch.

Logan's whole body arched backward as if he'd been struck by lightning.

The heart monitor they'd clipped to his finger began to scream. His eyes, exposed for an instant in the gap under the faceplate, were rolled back white. His lips were going blue. He made a sound, a thin terrible gasping wheeze, and his hands clawed at the carpet...

"Put it back!" David was shouting. He didn't know he was shouting. "Put it back, put it back..."

The paramedic let go. The headset dropped back into place over Logan's eyes.

Logan sagged. His body went loose. On the monitor, the screaming stopped, and the jagged line steadied, and slowed, and settled back into that slow, slow, terrible rhythm. His lips pinked. His hands fell open on his knees again, palms up.

Very softly, he began to hum.

The two paramedics looked at each other. For a moment, neither of them said anything at all. The woman's face was white.

"Okay," she said quietly. "Okay. We leave it on. We take him in with it on." She looked at David. Her eyes were frightened. "I've been doing this nineteen years," she said. "I've never seen anything like that."

"Neither have I," said David.

✦

Claire met them at the emergency entrance of Vessey Regional at ten-twenty, already in scrubs, already shouting at somebody. She'd come straight from her house. She took one look at Logan on the gurney with the black headset burning orange on his face and her expression went perfectly flat and professional, the face she must have worn a thousand times over the bodies of a thousand broken strangers. But when she put her hand on David's arm, her fingers were shaking.

"Pediatric ICU," she said. "They've got the other six up there. It's a whole ward now. I'll get you in."

"Claire, he can't..."

"I know. I heard. The paramedics called it in. Nobody's touching that headset." She gripped his arm harder. "Davey, they tried to take Owen's off this afternoon. To do an MRI. It was the same thing. Exactly the same. His heart nearly stopped."

The elevator doors slid shut. The elevator had a screen in the corner above the buttons, a little display that showed the floor numbers and, between them, a scrolling message: WELCOME TO VESSEY REGIONAL MEDICAL CENTER. As the elevator rose, the message flickered. For an instant, it read WELCOME TO THE KINGDOM.

David turned his face to the wall. So did Claire.

✦

The pediatric ICU was at the end of a long corridor on the fourth floor, and it had been turned into something David didn't have a word for. The doors were propped open. There were too many people in the corridor: nurses and doctors and orderlies, parents sitting on the floor with their backs against the walls, a man in a county health department windbreaker talking urgently into a phone. There was a police officer standing by the nurses' station with his hat in his hands, looking lost.

Inside, the lights were dimmed. There were seven beds now, curtained off from each other, and in each bed there was a child, and on each child's face there was a headset.

They were all different headsets. Cheap ones and expensive ones, white and black and gray, one with stickers of cartoon cats all over the faceplate. But every one of them had the same light burning on it. Hard, steady amber.

And every child was humming. All of them. Very softly. The same five notes going up and the same five notes coming down, in perfect unison, like a choir.

David stood in the doorway and listened to it, and something in his stomach turned to water.

They put Logan in the last bed by the window. A nurse with a kind tired face hooked him up to an IV and a heart monitor and a little clip on his finger that glowed red. She didn't try to touch the headset. Nobody did. Claire stood at the foot of the bed with her arms folded and watched every move the nurse made like a hawk.

In the next bed, behind a half-drawn curtain, David could see a boy with a faint pink ghost of a painted smile still on his cheeks. Owen. His mother sat beside him in a plastic chair, holding his limp hand, staring at nothing. She looked up as they settled Logan in. She looked at David.

He waited for her to scream at him. To blame him. He would have deserved it.

She didn't. She just looked at Logan in the next bed, at the black headset on his face, burning, and her own face crumpled.

"Yours too," she said softly.

"Mine too."

She nodded. She looked back at Owen. After a while, without looking at David, she reached out with her free hand, across the gap between the two beds, and pulled the curtain all the way open, so that the two boys lay side by side.

"They should be together," she said. "They're best friends."

✦

At midnight, the doctors came. A pediatric neurologist who'd driven down from Columbus, a young resident with dark circles under her eyes, a psychiatrist who seemed to have no idea why he'd been called. They examined Logan the way they'd examined the others, carefully, around the edges of the headset. They checked his reflexes and his blood and his heart. They found nothing wrong. His brain activity, when they finally got an EEG cap fitted under the headset's strap, was the strangest thing the neurologist had ever seen, "like REM sleep, but continuous, and coordinated," she said, frowning at the printout, "as if he's watching something very intently." But there was no damage, no infection, no toxin. Only a heart rate that hovered around forty-five and a body temperature two degrees below normal and a boy who would not, could not, wake up.

"It's the same in all of them," the neurologist said to David in the corridor afterward. She looked shaken. "Every one. Same heart rate, same temperature, same EEG. The same, Mr. Kerrigan. I've never seen seven patients so identical. It's as though they're all..." She hesitated.

"In the same place," David said.

She looked at him. She didn't argue.

"We'll keep monitoring," she said. "We'll support them. Fluids, nutrition. If anything changes..." She trailed off. She didn't know what to say. Nobody did.

After she left, David went back into the dim ward and sat in the plastic chair beside Logan's bed. The humming filled the room like the sound of the sea. On the other side of Owen's bed, Simone Baptiste had fallen asleep sitting up, her head tipped against the wall, her son's hand still in hers. Claire had gone to argue with someone at the nurses' station about blankets.

David took Logan's cold hand. He held it in both of his.

"I'm here," he said. "Okay? I'm right here. I'm not going anywhere. I'm not getting on any calls."

The heart monitor beeped, slow and steady. Forty-four. Forty-five. Forty-four.

On the windowsill above the bed, somebody had left a laptop. A hospital laptop on a little rolling cart, there to display the EEG readings. Its screen had gone to sleep. In its dark glass, David could see the reflection of the dim ward behind him: the curtained beds, the children with their burning amber lights, the sleeping mothers.

And at the foot of Logan's bed, in the reflection, there stood a tall figure in a suit of two colors.

It was looking at David. It was smiling. It had always been smiling.

It raised a white-gloved hand.

David didn't look away. Not this time. He looked straight at the reflection in the dark laptop screen, at that wide red painted smile, and he said, very quietly, so as not to wake anyone, "What do you want?"

The laptop woke up.

The EEG display was gone. Instead, the screen showed a view. A wide, steep bank of wooden bleachers, rising up and up into darkness under the swooping striped canvas of a circus tent. A spotlight. A sawdust ring. And in the very front row of the bleachers, sitting in a line, very straight, with their hands resting palms-up on their knees, were seven children. Owen. A little girl with braids. A boy David didn't know. Another girl. Two more. And at the end, on the aisle, in a gray hoodie, with his bare feet in the sawdust...

Logan. Watching the ring with wide, unblinking eyes.

Beside him, holding his hand, sat a woman in a muddy fleece vest.

A voice came out of the laptop's tinny speakers, warm and rich and friendly, the voice of somebody's favorite uncle.

"Come and play, Dad," said Mister Pocket. "The show's about to start. And we've saved you a seat."

Chapter Sixteen

Ward Six

By the morning of the second day there were eleven.

They came in through the night, one or two at a time, wheeled up from the emergency department on gurneys with their headsets burning amber and their parents running beside them. A girl of ten found sitting on the roof of a parked school bus. A pair of brothers, eight and eleven, found together on the shoulder of Route 9 holding hands, walking south toward the reservoir. A boy from St. Bartholomew's who had painted his face with his mother's lipstick. The hospital gave up trying to fit them all in the pediatric ICU and opened an empty surgical ward on the sixth floor that had been closed for renovation, and the nurses, without anyone telling them to, started calling it Ward Six.

David hadn't left. He'd slept, if it was sleeping, in the plastic chair beside Logan's bed, his head tipped back against the wall. When they moved the children upstairs, he walked beside Logan's gurney with one hand on the rail, and in the elevator he kept his eyes on the floor.

Ward Six was a long, bare room with a scuffed linoleum floor and a row of tall windows down one side, looking out over the hospital parking lot to the brown hills beyond. Somebody had pushed the beds into two rows, facing each other across the center aisle. Somebody else, at some point in the night, had taped sheets of brown paper over every window and every screen in the room. Claire, David guessed, though when he asked her she only shrugged and said, "Some of the parents saw things."

The laptop with the feed was still there. It sat on its rolling cart at the end of the ward, and nobody turned it off. Nobody could. They'd tried. They'd shut its lid and unplugged it and taken out its battery and carried it down the hall to a supply closet, and an hour later it had been back on its cart at the end of Ward Six with its lid open and its screen glowing, and none of the night staff would admit to having moved it.

So now it stayed, and they took turns watching it, because not watching it was worse.

✦

It showed the Big Top. Always the Big Top. The view never changed: a high wide angle from somewhere up near the top of the tent, looking down past the swooping striped canvas and the dangling ropes of an empty trapeze rig, at a sawdust ring lit by a single hard white spotlight. And beyond the ring, the bleachers.

The bleachers went up and up and up, into darkness, much farther than any real tent could have held. And they were filling.

On the first night, there had been seven children in the front row. Now there were eleven, sitting in a line, very straight, with their hands on their knees and their faces turned toward the empty ring. And behind them, in the second row and the third and the tenth, faint as ghosts, as breath on a window, there were other figures. Hundreds of them. Pale and blurred like the people in old photographs, the ghost-crowd David had seen drifting across the plaza on his very first day. They were sitting in the bleachers now. Waiting.

Everybody comes, the clown had told Logan. Everybody.

David sat in front of the laptop for a long time that morning, studying it with the cold, flat, focused attention he'd spent fifteen years training. He watched it the way he'd watch a level he was trying to break. He looked for seams.

He found three.

The first was the woman. She sat beside Logan at the end of the front row, holding his hand, and she wore Nora's face and Nora's fleece and Nora's lanyard full of keys. But she didn't move like Nora. That was the seam. She didn't fidget. Nora had never sat still in her life; she'd jiggled her knee through every movie, every dinner, every wedding. This thing sat as still as a mannequin, and every now and then it turned its head and smiled at Logan, and the smile was the same each time. Precisely the same. Like a loop.

The second seam was at the back of the tent.

He almost missed it. It was in the darkness behind the highest rows of bleachers, where the canvas met the ground, and he only saw it because his eyes had been trained for years to look in the places where the light didn't reach. Shapes. Big, dark, hunched shapes, sitting in a line along the back wall of the tent. Five of them. One of them, the biggest, had a broad back that caught the faint spill of the spotlight like frost.

They were sitting with their backs turned to the ring.

And beside the smallest of them, so small David had to lean close to the screen to see it, there was a child. Not sitting in the bleachers with the others. Crouched in the dark at the back with the gorillas, his face turned away from the ring like theirs, his skinny arms wrapped around his knees. A cowlick stuck up from the crown of his head.

"Danny," David whispered.

The third seam was the clock.

It was painted on a banner that hung above the entrance to the ring, a big round circus clock with ornate Roman numerals and two gilded hands. He hadn't noticed it on the first night. Or it hadn't been there. Now the hands stood at a quarter past ten. When he looked again, two hours later, they stood at a quarter to eleven.

It was counting down.

"To what?" Claire asked, when he showed her. She'd come up from the ER on her break, still in scrubs, and stood behind his chair drinking coffee from a paper cup.

"Midnight," David said. "The show starts at midnight." He'd done the math on a paper napkin. Two hours on the painted clock for every... he checked his watch... every eighteen hours in the real world, roughly. It wasn't exact. It didn't need to be. "Midnight on the clock is about the night of the thirtieth. Thursday night."

Claire set down her coffee. "The thirtieth."

"The anniversary of the fire."

They looked at each other.

"And what happens at midnight?" Claire said very quietly.

David looked at the screen, at the line of children in the front row with their hands on their knees.

"I don't know," he said. "But I think when the show starts, they don't get to leave."

✦

On the second afternoon, Rosa came.

She came up to Ward Six in her muddy fleece with her lanyard full of keys, and for a split second, when she came through the door, David's heart stopped, because the fleece and the lanyard were so much like... But it was Rosa, square and silver-haired and furious, and she walked straight down the aisle between the two rows of beds and stopped at the foot of Logan's and looked at him for a long time without saying anything.

Then she sat down on the edge of the bed and put her hand on Logan's ankle under the blanket and said to David, without looking at him, "Hattie's sick."

"Sick how?"

"Not eating. Not drinking. She hasn't moved from the corner of her night house in two days. She's got her face to the wall and her hands over her eyes." Rosa's voice was very flat. "The vet thinks it's her heart. She thinks it might be the end. I think..." She stopped. "I think she's hiding. I think she's doing what she told us to do. I think she knows."

"Knows what?"

"Whatever's happening to these kids. She's been through it before." Rosa's hand tightened on Logan's ankle. "She's fifty-two, David. She's got congestive heart failure. She can't hide in a corner for three days without food. It'll kill her."

David didn't answer. He didn't know what to say.

Rosa was silent too, for a long time. Then she reached into the pocket of her fleece and took out a thing David didn't expect: a small black external hard drive, scuffed and dented, with a strip of masking tape on top. On the tape, in a quick slanting impatient hand: N.K. — VIDEOS — HATTIE + MISC.

"She kept these on the office computer," Rosa said. "Years of footage. Hattie signing, mostly. Training sessions, enrichment. But there's other stuff on there. Personal stuff. She'd film herself sometimes, talking through her notes. And there's..." She stopped. "There's some of Logan. When he was little. With her and Hattie."

David took it. He held it in his hands.

"I didn't know what else to bring," Rosa said. "I just thought... if that thing is going to use her face, then he should get to see the real one."

✦

That night, at two in the morning, David's phone rang.

It shouldn't have. It was in his coat pocket in a sealed metal tin, the cookie tin from the oven, which he'd brought from home in a plastic bag because he didn't know what else to do with it. He'd turned the phone off before he sealed the tin. It rang anyway, muffled, a tinny little electronic jingle that wasn't his ringtone.

Five notes up. Five down.

Half the parents in Ward Six sat up in their chairs.

Chapter Seventeen

The Way In

Rosa made a sound in her throat.

"The fire," he said.

Wendell closed his eyes.

He looked like a dead thing. The paint was flaking off his face. The apes wouldn't look at him at all anymore; Bosun had taught them all to turn their backs. Even Hattie, the little one. "Cyril died in it." Wendell opened his eyes. "Cyril Mott, the nobody, the man with the forgettable face. He burned with the tent. But I'm not sure the thing in him did. Nobody spoke.

"Oh God," Rosa said softly.

"And then you." Wendell's eyes were red. "I didn't choose you. He did. He went through my list of testers and read all of them, everything about them, everything he could find. And he picked you. "Can you shut it off?" Claire said. "Just... pull the plug? Wipe the server? Burn the house down?"

"You saw what happens when you take the headset off one child." Wendell looked at her. "Their minds are in there, Ms. Kerrigan. Their attention. That's what he feeds on, and that's what he's holding. "Broken how?"

"I don't know. Not exactly. But you know it, don't you? Your wife knew it. Hattie knew it." Wendell's voice shook. "Bosun knew it, thirty-seven years ago, sitting in the middle of the ring with his back turned to him. Every time an audience stopped looking, he got smaller. Every time. He can't stand not being seen. If enough of them turned away at once, if the whole house turned its back..."

"He'd starve," David said.

"Or he'd let go." Wendell's hands were clenched on the black ledger. "I don't know which. I don't think anybody does. But it's the only weakness he's ever had."

David looked at Rosa. She was watching him with her arms folded and her jaw set, and he could see in her face that she'd already worked it all out, and already decided she wasn't going to try to stop him.

"Davey," she said. "No."

From the cellar. The rig I built it on. It's wired straight into the core. It's the closest thing there is to a back door."

"And from there you can see what I see? You can help?"

"I can watch," said Wendell Mott, and his voice was like ash. "That's the one thing I've always been good at."

Chapter Eighteen

Second Headset

They left the hospital at four o'clock on Thursday afternoon, the thirtieth of October, with eight hours to go.

Chapter Nineteen

The Midway

The fountain was dry. The three stone elephants stood in a basin of cracked tiles filled with ash, and their trunks had been broken off and laid at their feet, neatly, side by side, like tools set out on a bench. Where the water should have come from, a thin dark trickle ran down each elephant's face from a hole between the eyes.

David didn't look at it for long. He kept to the left, the way Wendell had told him.

The plaza was empty. The carousel stood silent under its scorched canopy. As he passed, every one of them turned its head to follow him. He heard the creak of their necks on their brass poles. He didn't look.

Beyond the carousel, the long wooden chute of the log flume ran away into the dark toward the far end of the park, elevated on trestles, its water gone. Beside it, a narrow service path ran along the base of the trestles, half-hidden by blackened bushes. The safe way. He found it and followed it.

"Good," said Wendell in his ear. "Good. Keep going. It runs almost all the way to the back of the park. When I was a boy, he never came down here. He didn't like the flume. Too wet, too loud. Nobody could hear him over the water."

Away to his right, beyond the bushes, he could see the lights of the midway, strings of colored bulbs flickering and buzzing, and hear the calliope, faster now, a little out of tune, and over it, a sound that made the hair on his arms stand up: laughter. A crowd laughing. Hundreds of people, far away, laughing all together at something he couldn't see.

He'd gone about a hundred yards when the path ended.

It didn't end the way paths end. There was no wall, no fence, no gate. It simply stopped, as though somebody had cut it with scissors. One moment there was gravel under his feet and the trestles overhead, and the next there was a sheer, smooth, gray nothing, a flat blank surface like the side of a box, stretching up and away into the dark.

And on the gray nothing, painted in red letters, dripping:

NO SHORTCUTS, FRIEND.

"He's changed it," Wendell whispered. "That's... he's never changed the layout before. He's never been able to..."

"He's stronger now," said David. "Eleven kids. He's eating well."

Laughter drifted over from the midway. Closer.

David put his hand against the gray surface. It was cold, and perfectly smooth, and it didn't give. He pressed harder. Nothing.

"Wendell. The only way to the Big Top from here is the midway?"

"Yes. Through the midway, past the games, past the funhouse. Then the Big Top's at the end. I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I thought..."

"It's okay." David turned around. The service path stretched back the way he'd come, toward the carousel and the dead fountain and the gate. To his left were the trestles of the flume. To his right, the black bushes, and beyond them the buzzing lights.

He thought about seams.

On the second day in this place, a sunlit Wednesday a lifetime ago, he'd found his first bug: popcorn hovering an inch above the bottom of its glass case. A tiny thing. A physics fudge. Somebody had set the popcorn's collision box a fraction too high, and the game had settled it on an invisible floor that wasn't quite the real floor. Things that are made have seams. That was the first rule. And the second was: The world only exists where someone has bothered to build it.

Mister Pocket had changed the path. He'd put up a wall. But walls in a simulation weren't walls. They were surfaces, painted on nothing, with nothing behind them. And everything in this park had been built by one man, by hand, from a child's memory. A child's memory had edges. A child's memory had places it forgot.

"Wendell," David said. "When you built the flume. The water channel. What's under it?"

A pause. "Under it? The trestles. The ground."

"No. Under the water. When it had water. What did you build underneath the surface of the water in the channel?"

A longer pause. Then, slowly: "Nothing. It's a shader. A water surface. There's... there's a floor under it, I think, a collision plane, so the logs float. But the channel bottom... I never modeled it. You can't see it. Nobody would ever go there."

"Right," said David. "Nobody would ever go there."

He climbed the trestle.

Chapter Twenty-Two

Out There

Rosa Delgado had spent thirty-one years around animals that could kill her, and she had learned a few things in that time that weren't written in any manual.

She had learned that fear has a smell, and that every animal on earth can read it. She had learned that the most dangerous moment with any creature is not when it charges but in the silence just before, when it's deciding. She had learned that the worst injuries she'd ever seen came not from the big males with their teeth and their chest-beating, but from small, quiet, frightened things that had been backed into corners.

She was watching Wendell Mott now.

Chapter Twenty-Three

The Big Top

The tent was bigger on the inside.

David had known it would be. He'd seen it on the hospital laptop, the bleachers that rose and rose into darkness much farther than any real tent could hold. But knowing it and standing in it were different things. He came in through the flap and stopped dead in the sawdust, and the Big Top opened up around him like the inside of a cathedral, like the inside of a sky.

The canvas walls curved up and away on every side, striped red and yellow, red and yellow, faded and scorched at the seams, climbing toward a peak so high it was lost in shadow. Ropes and rigging hung down out of the dark like vines. An empty trapeze swung slowly back and forth, high up, creaking, though there was no wind. The air smelled of sawdust and greasepaint and old popcorn and, underneath, faintly, smoke.

And the bleachers. Rows of them, on every side of the ring, wooden planks on iron frames, rising up and up and up until they were lost in darkness too, and every row was full.

The ghost-crowd. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Pale and smeared and translucent, men and women and children in the clothes of 1979, 1983, 1988. Bell-bottoms and feathered hair and windbreakers and striped tube socks. They sat shoulder to shoulder in the dim light, motionless, faces turned toward the ring. Every one of them. Watching.

David realized, with a slow cold horror, who they were.

They were the audience. Every audience. Every person who'd ever sat in the bleachers of the Pocket Revue and laughed until they couldn't stop, and come out quiet. Everything he'd taken from them. Every piece he'd kept. Thirty-seven years of attention, hoarded in his pockets like coins, and spent tonight to fill the house.

Part of the show forever.

Marguerite had said she stood at the back once, and felt it pull at her like an undertow. David wondered if somewhere up in those rows there was a pale, smeared girl of ten or eleven with Claire's face, still sitting where she'd been left.

He didn't look for her. He looked at the ring.

It was a circle of sawdust, maybe forty feet across, ringed by a low painted wooden curb. A single spotlight shone down on it from somewhere up in the dark, a hard white column of light full of drifting dust. Above the entrance on the far side hung the banner with the big painted circus clock. Its gilded hands stood at two minutes to twelve.

And between David and the ring, at the bottom of the nearest bleachers, ran the front row.

Eleven children, sitting in a line, very straight, with their hands on their knees and their faces turned toward the spotlight. Owen, with the pink ghost of a painted smile on his cheeks. The little girl from St. Bartholomew's with lipstick smeared across her face. Lorraine Pruitt's grandson Mikey, in his socks. The two brothers, still holding hands. And at the near end, on the aisle, in a gray hoodie with his bare feet in the sawdust...

"Logan," David said.

His son didn't turn. He sat with his eyes fixed on the empty ring, unblinking, his face pale and thin and lit white by the spill of the spotlight. He was smiling a little. A small, dreamy, faraway smile. Beside him, holding his hand, sat the woman in the muddy fleece.

She turned her head and looked at David. She smiled. The same smile, exactly the same.

David started toward them.

He walked down the aisle along the bottom of the bleachers. It was perhaps thirty feet to the end of the front row, to Logan. He walked and walked. And after a minute he understood that he wasn't getting any closer. The front row stretched. With every step he took, it seemed to slide away from him, the way the midway had folded up like an accordion in his dream, except backward, unfolding, lengthening, so that the line of children was always just ahead, thirty feet, thirty feet, thirty feet.

He ran. It didn't help. The sawdust churned under his shoes, and the children stayed where they were, just out of reach.

"Logan! Logan!"

Logan didn't hear. Or didn't turn.

The calliope stopped.

One.

The spotlight swung. It left the ring and swept across the sawdust and up the bleachers and down again, searching, a column of hard white light full of dust, and it found David where he stood and stopped on him.

Two.

"Ladies and gentlemen," said a voice. "Boys and girls. Children of all ages."

And the ghost-crowd stirred. Every pale, smeared face in every row turned away from the ring, and toward the spotlight, and toward David. Thousands of them. Chapter Twenty-Four

What's in His Pocket

Chapter Twenty-Five

Turn Your Backs

Two watchers. Out of thousands. Two. And it had flickered.

He went down the row.

And David went the other way, to the brothers, and knelt in front of them, and took the older one's face in his hands, and said, "Hey. Hey, buddy. Look at me. Look at me instead." And the boy blinked, and saw him, and David said, "Now help your brother," and the older brother turned and put his hands on the younger one's face, and said, crying, "Charlie. Charlie, look at me," and the little one blinked too.

Behind them, the giant was screaming. Not words anymore. A raw, scraping, furious shriek like a nail down glass, and the sound of enormous footsteps, of things smashing, of the bleachers splintering under huge red shoes. When nobody's looking at him, he can't see anything either. He gets all blind and small.

Mikey Pruitt was the last. David reached him at the same moment Logan did, from the other side, and they knelt in front of him together, and Logan said, "Hey, Mikey, your great-uncle's here. Danny. He's been looking out for you. Did you know that?" and Mikey blinked, and looked at him, and burst into tears.

Eleven children stood in the sawdust of the Big Top with their backs to the ring.

The spotlight was the color of weak tea.

But it wasn't enough.

David knew it at once. He could feel it at his back, a pressure, a heat, like standing with your back to a bonfire. The thing in the ring was smaller. It had to be smaller. But it was still there, still burning, still being fed.

One by one, row by row, the pale smeared faces turned from the ring and saw the apes, and the boy, and the children standing in the sawdust below with their backs turned, and something in them seemed to remember. Seemed to wake.

When Bosun did it, Marguerite had said, the crowd would stop laughing. Every time. Like a spell breaking.

The spotlight went out.

✦

In the darkness, something began to shrink.

David heard it. He didn't turn to look, and neither did anyone else. But he heard it, behind them, in the ring. A sound like air rushing out of something enormous. A long, thin, dwindling wail. And another sound, under it: a soft pattering, like rain, like confetti falling.

A baseball card. A child's barrette. A ring. A plain gold wedding ring.

The pockets had burst. Everything he'd ever kept was falling out.

And as each thing fell, somewhere in the dark stands, a ghost sighed. A soft, long, relieved breath, like someone setting down something heavy they'd been carrying for a very long time. And then they were gone. Not faded. Gone. Released. David felt them going, all around him in the darkness, one after another, like lights going out across a sleeping town.

"Please," it said.

"Please. Somebody. Somebody look at me. I can't... I can't see. It's so dark. Please. I don't want to be nothing again. I don't want to be nobody." The voice was thin and gray and ordinary, the voice of a man you could stand beside in a room for an hour and never remember. "Please. Just one of you. Just look."

Nobody looked.

"Son," whispered the voice in the dark. "Wendell. My boy. You're still there. I know you're still there. You've always been there. Don't leave me. Please. Don't leave your old man alone in the dark."

In the cold cellar under the caretaker's house, under the single bare bulb, Wendell Mott sat in front of the middle screen and stared.

Chapter Twenty-Six

Exit Through the Gift Shop

For a while, nothing at all happened.

They stood in the dark of the Big Top with their backs to the empty ring: eleven children, one man, four gorillas, and a boy in a striped shirt halfway up the splintered bleachers. Nobody moved. Nobody spoke. It was as if the whole tent were holding its breath, waiting to be sure. Waiting for a voice, or a laugh, or the creak of a giant red shoe in the sawdust.

None came.

Then, high up in the peak of the tent, a seam split.

David heard it before he saw it: a long, ripping, tearing sound, like a bedsheet torn in two. And through the gap in the canvas came light. Not the sick orange light of the burning sky. Not the hard white glare of the spotlight. Gray, soft, pale light. The light of very early morning. It fell in a long slanting shaft down through the dark, full of drifting dust, and laid itself across the sawdust of the ring.

Another seam split. Another. The canvas was coming apart. Long strips of red and yellow peeled away from the frame and drifted slowly down through the air like falling leaves, and as they fell, they faded, until they were only colors, and then not even that. The ropes and rigging dissolved. The empty trapeze swung once more and was gone. Through the widening holes in the roof David could see the sky, and it was no longer churning, no longer burning. It was the clean pale gray of a sky just before dawn, with one last star hanging low in it.

"I don't know. Minutes. You need to get to the gate. All of you. On your own feet. That's the only way out."

David turned to the children.

They were standing in a ragged cluster in the sawdust, blinking up at the dissolving roof. They looked very small. Barefoot, pajama'd, painted, frightened. Waiting.

It was beautiful, in its way. That was what David would remember, afterward. Not frightening anymore. Just ending. The burned midway was dissolving into the dawn like a sandcastle into the tide. The game booths were melting softly into the gray air, their strings of colored bulbs going out one by one and drifting away like dandelion seeds. The Banana Hut was a faint yellow shimmer. Chapter Twenty-Seven

Power Down

Wendell burned the ledger three days later.

They did it in the ruins, at the foot of the hill below the skeleton of the gorilla house, on a cold bright afternoon with the wind coming down out of the northwest and the last leaves gone from the trees. David, Rosa, and Wendell. Logan had wanted to come. David had said no, and Logan had looked at him for a long moment and then said, "Okay. That's fair," which was so unexpected that David had almost changed his mind.

Wendell had wiped the servers the night before. All of them. He'd sat in the cellar for nine hours, writing zeros over every sector of every drive, three passes, then seven, then pulled the drives out of the rack and taken them to a scrapyard in Marietta and watched them go into a shredder. The headsets, all of them, the black one and Logan's cracked secondhand one and the eleven from Ward Six that the parents had been glad to hand over, went into the shredder after them. "Probably unnecessary," Wendell had said. "Probably." And had watched until the last one was gone.

The ledger was the last thing.

He held it in his hands for a long time at the foot of the hill, the cracked black leather, the red-edged pages, his father's small dense machine-perfect handwriting. Then he set it down on a flat stone and poured lighter fluid on it and struck a match.

The pages curled. Blackened. The fire took them quickly, as if it had been waiting.

Nobody watched.

That was Rosa's idea. As soon as the match caught, she turned around, and put her back to the fire, and after a moment David did too, and then, slowly, so did Wendell. The three of them stood at the foot of the hill with their backs to the burning book, looking up at the skeleton of the green glass dome against the hard blue sky, and listened to the pages crackle and hiss behind them.

It took a long time. When the crackling finally stopped, Wendell let out a breath that seemed to go on forever.

"I keep thinking I should feel something," he said. "Grief. Or relief. Something."

"What do you feel?" Rosa asked.

Wendell thought about it.

"Tired," he said. "I feel so tired. I think I'd like to sleep for about a year." He looked up at the iron ribs of the dome. "I haven't slept a full night since I was five years old."

Rosa put a hand on his arm. "Then go home and sleep, Wendell."

"I don't have a home. I have a house full of screens."

"Then take the screens down," she said. "And sleep in it anyway."

David went to Orchard Street on Sunday.

Lorraine Pruitt opened the door before he knocked. She'd been watching for him from the window. Mikey was in the front room behind her, sitting on the floor with a box of old Matchbox cars, rolling them along the carpet, making engine noises. He looked up at David, and his face went still for a moment, as if he were trying to remember something. Then he smiled shyly and went back to his cars.

"He doesn't remember any of it," Lorraine said quietly in the kitchen. "The doctors say that's normal. Trauma. He says he had a long dream about a park, and some gorillas, and a boy in a stripey shirt who was nice to him. That's all." She poured coffee. Her hands were steady. "He asked me yesterday who Danny was. I didn't know what to tell him."

"Tell him the truth," David said. "Tell him Danny was his great-uncle, who loved gorillas, and who looked after him when he was lost."

Lorraine looked at him.

He told her the rest. He told her about the gorilla house in the green summer night, and the boy on the low branch, swinging his legs. He told her about the Polaroid, and the boy crying into his scabbed arms, and Mabel holding him. He told her about the bleachers, and a thin high voice shouting Turn around! to two thousand ghosts. And the gate, and the dawn, and the little blue car.

"He said to tell you that you weren't late," David said. "He said to tell you it's okay. He said he was having the best time."

Lorraine Pruitt sat at her cherry-print tablecloth with her hands around her coffee mug and didn't move for a long time. Then she got up and went to the front window and stood looking out at the porch, where a light fixture hung beside the door, its bulb burned out years ago.

"Mom left that light on every night for twenty-six years," she said. "After she died, I couldn't bring myself to keep it on. And I couldn't bring myself to change the bulb. So it's just been dead." She was quiet. "I think I'll change it now. And I think I'll turn it off."

That evening, Rosa called the house on Bell Street on the beige kitchen phone.

"She came in," she said, before David could say hello. "Hattie. She came in from the glass last night, by herself, and went to her nest in the night house. And this morning she ate. All of it. Kiwi, sweet potato, the browse, everything. She took her meds. The vet came out and listened to her heart and says it's no worse than it was a month ago. She says she doesn't understand it." A pause. Rosa's voice changed. "The others came in with her. Kito and Dot and the babies. They're all sleeping together in one big nest. They've never done that before."

"Rosa..."

"Bring Logan out," Rosa said. "Tomorrow. And bring Lorraine Pruitt, if she'll come. Hattie's been signing all day. The same thing, over and over. I think she wants to say it to someone who'll understand."

"What's she signing?"

There was a silence on the line, full of something David couldn't name.

"BOY SAFE," said Rosa. "BOY SAFE. BOY SAFE. BOY SAFE."

Chapter Twenty-Eight

Daylight

They went out to Halloway on a Monday in early November, in Claire's car, with the windows cracked to the cold and Lorraine Pruitt in the back seat beside Logan, holding a faded Polaroid in both hands.

Hattie was waiting at the glass.

She was sitting in the grass in front of it, in the pale sun, wrapped in an old green blanket that Felix had given her and that she had, to everyone's astonishment, kept. The other four sat a little way behind her: Kito, Dot, Bean, and Pip, close together, grooming. When she saw them coming down the path from the visitor center, Hattie raised her head and made the low, soft, rumbling purr. The contented engine.

Logan went to the glass and put his palm against it. Hattie put her burned hand over his. She looked at the space beside his head. He looked at the space beside hers.

Then Lorraine came up beside him, slowly, as if she weren't sure she was allowed. She was fifty-two years old. She had not stood at a pane of glass with a gorilla on the other side of it since she was fourteen. She held up the Polaroid.

Hattie looked at it for a long time.

Then she took her hand off the glass over Logan's and moved it, slowly, to the place where Lorraine's fingers were holding the photograph. She pressed her palm flat there. Her eyes were wet.

She signed BOY. Then SAFE. Then a sign that Rosa, standing behind them with her arms folded and tears running openly down her weathered cheeks, had to think about for a moment before she said, very softly, "HOME."

BOY SAFE HOME.

Lorraine Pruitt put her forehead against the glass and cried, and the old gorilla on the other side put her own heavy gray forehead against it too, so that they were resting together, with only an inch of glass between them, the way a laughing boy and a baby gorilla had rested in the summer of 1988.

David stood back with Claire and let them.

After a while, Logan came and stood beside him. He leaned against his father's side. He didn't say anything. David put an arm around him, and Logan let it stay.

"Danny said to tell her something," Logan said quietly. "Can I?"

"Go ahead."

Logan went back to the glass. He crouched down in front of Hattie and looked at the grass a little to the side of her. He didn't know the signs. So he just said it.

"Bosun says you did good."

Hattie went very still. Then she turned around, slowly, deliberately, and sat with her back to the glass, facing out over her habitat, the oaks and the pond and the two acres of grass in the pale November sun. Her shoulders were heaving.

"Is she okay?" Logan whispered.

Rosa wiped her face with the back of her wrist.

"I think," she said, "she's just looking at where she lives."

Winter came, and went.

It was a hard one, by Ohio standards. Snow in December and again in January, deep enough that the schools closed for a week and the river froze at the edges and the Dairy Barn put up a sign that said CLOSED TILL SPRING, SORRY MARGUERITE. David drove out to Riverbend anyway, every other Sunday, with a large coffee milkshake from the gas station on Route 50, which Marguerite declared to be "barely adequate" and drank to the last drop. She asked him every time how the boy was. He told her. She listened with her huge magnified eyes on the frozen river, and nodded, and once, in February, she reached out and patted his hand and said, "You've got your daddy's ears. But you look at people now. Your daddy never did that. He was a good man, but he never looked."

He'd stopped sleeping on the couch.

It hadn't happened all at once. It had taken most of November. But one night he'd gone up the stairs (third, fifth, ninth, all of them groaning, cracking and whining under him; he still didn't know about the rule) and into the bedroom he'd shared with Nora for fourteen years, and he'd lain down on her side of the bed, and he'd slept until seven. After that, it got easier.

He worked less. He hadn't planned it. He'd simply started closing the laptop at five, and then at four-thirty on Fridays, and then he'd turned down a contract in January because it would have meant working through Logan's science fair, and found that the world didn't end. Money was tighter. They ate a lot of spaghetti. Logan said he didn't mind.

He'd started telling stories again. Not every night. But often. About Nora, mostly. About the gas station and the capuchin named Chip, which Logan made him tell three more times that winter, and about the snowstorm and the gorilla formula and the chest-beating in the delivery room. And Logan told stories back. About school. About Owen. About a girl in his science class named Priya who had a pet hedgehog and who, Logan reported with elaborate casualness, "is actually kind of okay, I guess." David had looked at him across the dinner table, and Logan had gone bright red, and they had both started laughing so hard that spaghetti came out of Logan's nose.

They did not have a headset in the house. Neither of them had suggested it. Logan's video channel, with its eleven subscribers, quietly went dark. He took up drawing instead, in a black-and-white composition book that Wendell had mailed him at Christmas with a note that said only, For maps. Or anything else. —W. Most of what Logan drew was gorillas.

Wendell himself came out to Halloway in March. It was the first time anyone in Vessey had seen him in a store, at a gas pump, in the daylight, in thirty-seven years. He'd taken the screens down. All of them. Rosa had gone out to help him and reported that the caretaker's house was full of daylight now, and very empty, and that he'd bought a single armchair for the front room and put it by the window, facing out at the trees. He'd sold the satellite dish. He said he was sleeping eight hours a night. He said it like a man describing a miracle.

He stood at the glass of the gorilla habitat for a long time that day, with his hands in the pockets of a new coat, not a cardigan. Hattie came over and looked at him. Not at his face. At the space beside his head. And Wendell, slowly, for the first time in his life, looked at the space beside hers.

She purred.

Spring came early, in the end. By the second week of April, the oaks at Halloway were in fuzzy pale green leaf, and the pond was full of frogs, and the young gorillas spent all day chasing each other around the climbing frames in the long warm afternoons.

Logan volunteered at the sanctuary on Saturdays now. He was too young to work with the animals directly, but Rosa had found him jobs: scrubbing feed buckets, chopping fruit, raking the capuchin island. And sorting donations. Halloway got a lot of donations, people dropping off cardboard boxes of old blankets and towels and toys, because the primates loved toys. Plastic balls, rubber rings, stuffed animals with the eyes removed, anything that could be poked or chewed or carried around or worn as a hat. Somebody had to go through the boxes and pull out anything that might be dangerous: anything too small, anything sharp, anything with batteries or buttons or loose parts that could be swallowed. That was Logan's job. He was good at it. He was patient. He found things that other people missed.

On the last Saturday in April, he was sitting at a picnic table outside the gorilla building in the sun, sorting a box of toys that had come in that morning, when he found the clown.

It was very small. A little tin figure, no bigger than his thumb, the kind of cheap painted toy that used to come in cereal boxes or on top of birthday cakes a long time ago. Its paint was chipped. It had a little top hat, and a ruffled collar, and a suit that was one color down one side and another color down the other, though the colors had faded so much it was hard to tell what they'd been. And it had a face. A white face, and two tiny blue diamonds over the eyes, and a smile. A painted red smile, very wide, wider than the tiny tin mouth beneath it, reaching almost to where its ears should be.

Logan held it in his palm and looked down at it.

It was a toy. Just an old toy. It had probably come from somebody's attic, from a box of a grandmother's things, from a yard sale. There were a million little tin clowns in the world. It didn't mean anything.

He felt something anyway. A cold little prickle on the back of his neck, in the warm April sun.

Behind him, on the other side of the laminated glass, something moved.

He turned his head, just a little. Not all the way. Out of the corner of his eye, he could see Hattie. She'd been sitting under the big oak in her nest of straw, dozing in the sun, wrapped in her old green blanket. Now she was sitting up. Her head was turned toward the picnic table. Toward Logan. Toward the small thing in his open hand.

And as he watched, slowly, deliberately, with great dignity, the old gorilla turned around.

She sat with her back to him. With her back to the little tin clown. Facing out over the grass and the oaks and the pond full of frogs, toward the far end of her habitat, where Kito and Dot and the two little ones were playing in the long gold light of the afternoon.

Logan smiled.

He closed his hand over the little clown. He didn't look at it again. He stood up from the picnic table and walked, without hurrying, to the big steel dumpster at the end of the gorilla building, the one that went out every Tuesday to the county landfill, and he dropped the little tin figure over the side without looking down. He heard it rattle against the bottom, once. And then, so faintly that he would never be sure afterward whether he'd heard it at all, or whether it was just the wind moving through the oaks, he heard something else.

Five notes. Going up.

He didn't wait for the five coming down. He turned his back on the dumpster, and walked away across the grass in the sun, and went to stand beside the glass, beside the old gray gorilla who was sitting with her back turned. He sat down in the grass on his side of the glass and turned his back too, and looked out with her at the trees, and the pond, and the young gorillas playing in the light.

After a while, his father came down the path from the visitor center with two cups of lemonade, the real kind, from the stand by the gate. He saw his son sitting with his back to the glass and the old gorilla sitting with her back to it on the other side, and he didn't ask why. He sat down in the grass beside Logan and handed him a lemonade and turned his back too.

The three of them sat there together for a long time, in the warm April sun, looking at where they lived.

Behind them, in the bottom of the dumpster, a small tin thing with a painted smile lay face-up in the dark, waiting to be seen.

Nobody looked.
